## Items

### 1. Almost-orthogonal vector capacity (qualitative JL claim)
- **statement**: Only n vectors can be exactly mutually orthogonal in an n-dimensional space, but exponentially many (in n) vectors can be chosen to be pairwise 'almost orthogonal' — cosine similarity below some threshold ε. This, plus the compressed-sensing fact that a sparse vector can be recovered from a lower-dimensional linear projection, is offered as the two mathematical intuitions that make superposition plausible.
- **equation_verbatim**: "it's only possible to have n orthogonal vectors in an n-dimensional space, [but] it's possible to have exp(n) many \"almost orthogonal\" (< ϵ cosine similarity) vectors in high-dimensional spaces. See the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma." [prose statement; no equation is printed for this claim anywhere in the paper]
- **symbols**: n = ambient dimension of the vector space; ϵ = the cosine-similarity threshold that defines "almost orthogonal" (unnamed/unbound in the source — no explicit dependence of the exp(n) count on ϵ is given here).
- **assumptions**: The space is "high-dimensional" (n large); "almost orthogonal" is measured by cosine similarity, not by any other angle/distance metric; the JL lemma itself is invoked by name, not restated.
- **derivation_steps**: Source states without derivation. The paper gives no proof, no explicit exp(n) formula with the ϵ-dependence spelled out, and no citation-internal equation — it is a one-sentence pointer to "the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma" as an external, well-known result. Immediately after, the paper pairs this with a second, likewise unproved heuristic (compressed sensing: sparse vectors can be recovered after projection to a lower dimension) and moves on to build its own toy model rather than deriving either bound.
- **worked_numbers**: none given in this paper. (The quantitative version — the constant C and the explicit e^{Cdδ²} count — is supplied instead by Engels et al. 2024's Appendix A; see the next item.)

### 2. Packing bound for pairwise δ-orthogonal low-dimensional feature subspaces (JL-lemma-style capacity theorem)
- **statement**: Engels et al. make the JL-style capacity claim rigorous and extend it from 1-dimensional feature directions to d'-dimensional feature SUBSPACES (needed for their multi-dimensional superposition hypothesis). They prove a two-sided bound: the number of pairwise δ-orthogonal d'-dimensional projection matrices that fit in a d-dimensional ambient space is exponential in d from below, but the best known upper bound is also exponential — with a large gap between the two.
- **equation_verbatim**: Base case (JL lemma, as invoked): "for any d and δ, we can choose e^{Cdδ^2} δ-orthogonal unit vectors in R^d indexed as v_i, for some constant C."
Theorem 1. "For any d′ and δ, it is possible to choose (1/d_max) e^{C1(d/d′)^2 δ^2} pairwise δ-orthogonal matrices A_i ∈ R^{n_i×d′} for some constant C1. Furthermore, it is not possible to choose more than e^{C2(d−d_max)δ log(1/δ)} for some constant C2."
Upper-bound closing step: "k e^{C d_max δ^2 log(1/δ)} < e^{Cdδ^2 log(1/δ)}" simplifying to "k < e^{C(d−d_max)δ^2 log(1/δ)}"
- **symbols**: d = ambient dimension of the representation space; d′ (= d_max in the theorem, the worst-case value taken over all feature subspace dimensions n_i) = dimension of each feature's subspace; δ = the orthogonality tolerance, defined via Definition 4: two matrices A_1∈R^{d×d1}, A_2∈R^{d×d2} are δ-orthogonal if |x_1·x_2| ≤ δ for ALL unit vectors x_1 in colspace(A_1), x_2 in colspace(A_2); n_i = number of columns (≤ d_max) actually used by the i-th matrix A_i; v_i = the i-th JL-guaranteed δ-orthogonal unit vector in R^d; C, C1, C2 = unspecified positive constants; k = the count being upper-bounded in the closing step. NOTE ON TRANSCRIPTION: the printed Theorem 1 statement suffered pdftotext sub/superscript reflow garbling (stacked exponents split across lines); the exponent structure above is reconstructed from the theorem's own proof body (which extracted cleanly) rather than invented — flagged explicitly rather than guessed, per instructions.
- **assumptions**: δ ∈ (0,1); all vectors/matrices are unit-norm / have unit-norm columns; the ambient inner product is the standard Euclidean one; the construction assumes the worst case that every feature subspace has dimension exactly d_max (the paper notes real models likely have many lower-dimensional features, so the true capacity is probably better than this worst-case bound).
- **derivation_steps**: The paper gives a complete, numbered first-principles proof, in three stages: (1) LEMMA 1 (coefficient-norm bound): given n pairwise δ-orthogonal unit vectors v_1..v_n forming A=[v_1,...,v_n], and a unit vector y=Az for coefficient vector z, bound ||z||_1. Steps given verbatim: (i) singular-value inequality gives ||z||_2 ≤ ||y||_2/σ_n = 1/σ_n where σ_n is the minimum singular value of A; (ii) since A has unit-norm columns, A^T A has all-ones diagonal and off-diagonal entries in [−δ,δ]; by the Gershgorin circle theorem all eigenvalues of A^T A lie in (1−δ(n−1), 1+δ(n−1)), so σ_n = sqrt(λ_min) ≥ sqrt(1−δ(n−1)); (iii) combine to get ||z||_2 ≤ 1/sqrt(1−δ(n−1)); (iv) on an n-hypersphere of L2-radius r, the maximum possible L1 norm is sqrt(n)·r (attained when all coordinates are equal), giving the stated result ||z||_1 ≤ sqrt(n/(1−δ(n−1))) ≤ sqrt(n/(1−δn)). (2) THEOREM 1 lower bound: apply the JL base case to get e^{Cdδ^2} pairwise δ-orthogonal unit vectors in R^d; partition them into consecutive blocks of size ≤ d_max to form (1/d_max)e^{Cdδ^2} matrices A_i; show any two such matrices are f(δ)-orthogonal for f(δ)=δd_max/(1−δd_max), via: triangle inequality on |y_i·y_j| for y_i,y_j in the two column spaces → bound each cross term |v_{k1}·u_{k2}| ≤ δ (they are individually δ-orthogonal JL vectors) → factor the double sum into (Σz_{i,k})(Σz_{j,k}) = ||z_i||_1 ||z_j||_1 → bound each factor by Lemma 1 → simplify using n_i,n_j ≤ d_max; then reparametrize δ ↦ f^{-1}(δ) = δ/(d_max(1+δ)) to convert an f(δ)-orthogonal family into a genuinely δ-orthogonal one, and use 1+δ ≤ 2 on (0,1) to simplify the resulting exponent, absorbing constants into C1. (3) THEOREM 1 upper bound: cites Alon (2003) for two external facts — a single d′-dimensional subspace can hold at most e^{Cd′^2 log(1/δ)} almost-orthogonal vectors, and the full d-dimensional ambient space cannot hold more than e^{Cdδ^2 log(1/δ)} δ-orthogonal vectors overall — then solves k·e^{Cd_max δ^2 log(1/δ)} < e^{Cdδ^2 log(1/δ)} for k, giving the stated upper bound. Every algebraic step in stages (1)–(2) is shown with a named justification ("Triangle Inequality", "All v_i,u_j are δ orthogonal", "Factoring the product", "By Lemma 1"); stage (3) is stated by citation to Alon (2003) rather than re-derived.
- **worked_numbers**: none numerically instantiated — C, C1, C2 are left as unspecified positive constants throughout. The paper's own qualitative reading of the result: "there is a large exponential gap between the lower and upper bound"; if the lower bound is close to tight, models would be strongly incentivized toward the smallest-dimensional feature encodings possible.

### 3. The two toy models (linear vs. ReLU-output) and the importance-weighted loss
- **statement**: Elhage et al.'s entire toy-model apparatus is built on two competing five-line models that differ only in whether the final layer has an activation function, trained on synthetic sparse data with per-feature importance weights.
- **equation_verbatim**: Linear Model:  h = Wx ,  x′ = W^T h + b  =  x′ = W^T W x + b
ReLU Output Model:  h = Wx ,  x′ = ReLU(W^T h + b)  =  x′ = ReLU(W^T W x + b)
THE LOSS:  L = Σ_x Σ_i I_i (x_i − x′_i)^2
- **symbols**: x ∈ R^n = the (synthetic) input feature vector, one coordinate per feature; W ∈ R^{m×n} (m ≪ n) = the down-projection matrix, whose i-th column W_i is the direction representing feature i in the m-dimensional bottleneck/hidden space; h = Wx = the compressed (bottleneck) representation; b = a learned bias vector; x′ = the model's reconstruction of x; I_i = the importance weight of feature i (a fixed positive scalar, not learned); the outer Σ_x sums (or, in the continuous-data reading, integrates) over the data distribution, Σ_i sums over the n features.
- **assumptions**: Each input coordinate x_i is independently zero with probability S_i (the sparsity of feature i) and otherwise drawn uniformly on [0,1]; the paper mostly restricts to the case where all features share one sparsity, S_i = S; the down-projection and up-projection share the same matrix (W and W^T — a deliberate, stated modeling choice to remove basis ambiguity, not a derived necessity); the two models are identical except for the final ReLU, isolating the activation function as the single cause of superposition.
- **derivation_steps**: source states the two models and the loss directly as the model definition (a construction, not something to be derived); the paper's stated MOTIVATION for each design choice (down-projection = linear map because superposition means each feature is a direction in the bottleneck; reuse W^T for the up-projection to remove ambiguity about which direction 'is' a feature; add a bias to let the model set unrepresented features to their expected value and to discard small interference noise; add ReLU because real networks apply an activation when features are used computationally) is given in prose immediately following, but is presented as motivation/rationale, not as a mathematical derivation from a stated objective.
- **worked_numbers**: Example configurations the paper actually trains: (i) n=20 features, m=5 hidden dims, I_i = 0.7^i (geometrically decaying importance) for the first visualization; (ii) n=400 features, m=30 hidden dims, I_i=1 for all i (uniform importance) for the 'dimensions per feature' experiment — the paper notes the exact n and m don't matter much as long as n ≫ m; (iii) n=5, m=2, I=1, activation density 1−S=0.05 (S=0.95) for the pentagon/non-uniform-superposition experiments.

### 5. Feature dimensionality D_i (fraction of a hidden dimension a feature occupies)
- **statement**: The paper defines a per-feature scalar, dimensionality D_i ∈ [0,1], that quantifies how much of a hidden dimension a given learned feature effectively occupies; features not learned get D_i=0, a feature with a dedicated axis gets D_i=1, and a feature sharing a hidden dimension antipodally with exactly one other feature gets D_i=1/2.
- **equation_verbatim**: D_i = ‖W_i‖^2 / Σ_j (Ŵ_i · W_j)^2 , where Ŵ_i is the unit version of W_i.
- **symbols**: W_i = the weight column (direction vector, in the m-dimensional hidden space) associated with feature i; Ŵ_i = W_i / ‖W_i‖, the unit vector in the same direction; the sum Σ_j ranges over ALL features j (including j=i, whose term (Ŵ_i·W_i)^2 = ‖W_i‖^2 reproduces the numerator).
- **assumptions**: W_i ≠ 0 (Ŵ_i undefined otherwise, but the formula is stated to give D_i=0 for unlearned features by convention/limit); the formula is basis-independent by construction, since it only involves dot products.
- **derivation_steps**: source states without derivation — presented directly as a definition, motivated only by the informal reading given afterward: the numerator ‖W_i‖^2 represents 'the extent to which a given feature is represented,' and the denominator represents 'how many features share the dimension it is embedded in,' obtained 'by projecting each feature onto its dimension.' The paper then works one worked example forward from the definition (the antipodal case) rather than deriving the definition itself from an optimality argument.
- **worked_numbers**: Antipodal pair: each of the two features has D = 1/(1+1) = 1/2 (numerator ‖W_i‖^2=1 for unit-norm W_i, denominator = 1 (self term) + 1 (the other, antipodal feature W_j=−W_i contributes (Ŵ_i·W_j)^2=1) = 2). Unlearned feature: D=0. The paper reports empirically that, when features are 'packed efficiently,' Σ_i D_i equals the number of embedding (hidden) dimensions m. Later ("WHY THESE GEOMETRIC STRUCTURES?"), the paper reports observed clustering of D_i at the specific fractions: 3/4 (tetrahedron), 2/3 (triangle), 1/2 (antipodal pair), 2/5 (pentagon), 3/8 (square antiprism), and 0 (feature not learned) — each fraction identified with a specific uniform-polytope vertex configuration.

### 6. D* = m/‖W‖²_F, the average "dimensions per feature" statistic
- **statement**: A convenient, basis-independent, aggregate way to measure how many features a trained model has learned to represent (relative to its hidden dimension) is the Frobenius-norm-based statistic D*, which the paper finds is empirically 'sticky' at the values 1 and 1/2 across a wide range of sparsity.
- **equation_verbatim**: D∗ = m / ‖W‖²_F
- **symbols**: m = number of hidden (bottleneck) dimensions; W = the full m×n weight matrix (all feature columns); ‖W‖²_F = the squared Frobenius norm of W, i.e. Σ_i ‖W_i‖².
- **assumptions**: Relies on the empirical fact (stated, not derived) that ‖W_i‖² ≈ 1 for a represented feature and ≈ 0 for an unrepresented one, which is what makes ‖W‖²_F ≈ (number of features represented); the statistic is explicitly noted to remain well-behaved ('behaves nicely') even in the dense regime S=0, where no privileged basis exists and features are represented along arbitrary (non-basis-aligned) directions, precisely because the Frobenius norm doesn't care about basis alignment.

- **worked_numbers**: The paper reports the plotted D* curve is empirically 'sticky' at D*=1 and D*=1/2 across a wide sparsity range, and links the 1/2 sticky point to models preferentially packing features into antipodal pairs (each pair sharing one hidden dimension, i.e. contributing 2 features per 1 dimension = D*=1/2 on average for that subpopulation).

### 8. Compressed-sensing capacity bound applied to the toy model: m = Ω(k log(n/k)) specialized via the sparsity parameter
- **statement**: The toy model's recoverability can be lower-bounded using classical compressed-sensing recovery bounds: an n-dimensional k-sparse vector can be recovered from an m-dimensional linear projection iff m scales as Ω(k log(n/k)); substituting the toy model's density-parameterized sparsity k=O((1−S)n) converts this into a bound purely in terms of the sparsity S and the number of hidden dimensions needed.
- **equation_verbatim**: "one can recover an n-dimensional k-sparse vector from an m dimensional projection if and only if m = Ω(k log(n/k)) [47, 48, 49]."
"the integer k for which all vectors have at most k non-zero entries is determined by the fixed density parameter S as k = O((1 − S)n). As a result, our bound is actually m = Ω(−n(1 − S) log(1 − S))."
- **symbols**: n = number of features (ambient dimension of the sparse vector being recovered); k = the sparsity level (max number of simultaneously-nonzero coordinates); m = number of hidden/bottleneck dimensions (the compressed-sensing measurement dimension); S = the per-coordinate zero-probability (feature sparsity parameter) used elsewhere in the paper; Ω(·), O(·) = standard asymptotic lower/upper bound notation.
- **assumptions**: The k-sparse-vector recovery bound is a classical compressed-sensing result cited from three external references [47,48,49], not derived in this paper; concentration of measure is invoked (not proven here) to justify that the toy model's i.i.d.-Bernoulli-sparsity vectors have a bounded number of nonzero entries WITH HIGH PROBABILITY, which is what licenses substituting a probabilistic sparsity model (S) for the compressed-sensing literature's worst-case sparsity count (k); the substitution converts a WORST-CASE compressed-sensing statement into a claim about the toy model, a step the paper itself flags as imperfect ("the connection is not entirely straightforward").
- **derivation_steps**: The substitution algebra from k=O((1−S)n) into m=Ω(k log(n/k)) is NOT spelled out step-by-step in the paper — the final substituted form m=Ω(−n(1−S)log(1−S)) is stated directly. The intermediate algebra the reader must supply: substituting k=(1−S)n into log(n/k) gives log(n/((1−S)n)) = log(1/(1−S)) = −log(1−S); multiplying by the leading (1−S)n factor gives m = Ω((1−S)n · (−log(1−S))) = Ω(−n(1−S)log(1−S)), matching the paper's stated result. This intermediate log-manipulation step is the survey's own responsibility to supply — the source jumps directly from the k-substitution to the final bound.
- **worked_numbers**: none numerically instantiated (S, n, m are left symbolic throughout); the paper's qualitative reading: the bound implies the number of representable features is LINEAR in m but modulated (scaled up) by sparsity — 'good news' for eliminating superposition in principle, but the bound also allows superposition to increase dramatically as sparsity S→1, which the authors hope is 'an artifact of the techniques in the proofs' rather than a fundamental barrier.

### 9. Polytope ↔ low-rank PSD matrix correspondence, and the regular-simplex "minimal superposition" construction
- **statement**: There is an exact correspondence between strategies for packing n features into m<n dimensions (i.e. rank-m matrices W^T W) and n-point polytopes in R^m; the paper uses this to show that, for equally-important equally-sparse features, projecting away from the fully-dense direction (1,1,1,...) is the geometrically 'minimal possible superposition' and yields a regular n-simplex.
- **equation_verbatim**: "If one has a rank-m n×n-matrix of the form W^T W, then W is a n×m-matrix. We can interpret the columns of W as n points in a m-dimensional space." "[G]iven a rank (n−i)-matrix of the form W^T W. We can characterize it by the dimensions W did not represent ... For example, if we start in three dimensions and then project such that W ⊥ (1, 1, 1), we get a triangle. More generally, setting W ⊥ (1, 1, 1, ...) gives us a regular n-simplex."
- **symbols**: W = n×m weight matrix (n features, m hidden dims, m<n); W^T W = the resulting n×n rank-m, symmetric, positive-semidefinite Gram-type matrix, whose off-diagonal entries encode pairwise feature-direction interference; W ⊥ v means the row space (equivalently, the discarded/unrepresented direction) of W is orthogonal to the vector v; (1,1,1,...) = the all-ones vector, i.e. the 'fully dense' direction corresponding to all features being simultaneously active.
- **assumptions**: Features are assumed equally important and equally sparse when arguing that projecting off (1,1,1,...) is optimal ('the best possible direction to not represent is the fully dense vector, assuming that features are equally important and sparse'); the correspondence itself (polytope ↔ rank-m PSD matrix) is asserted as an exact bidirectional correspondence, holding for ANY W, not only the uniform case.
- **derivation_steps**: The forward direction (W's columns = n points in R^m, and W^T W's off-diagonal geometry is 'driven by' the point geometry) is stated as a near-trivial observation, not formally proven — no explicit formula relating (W^T W)_{ij} to the geometric angle between points i and j is written out (though it follows immediately from (W^T W)_{ij}=W_i·W_j, which the paper does not spell out at this point). The reverse direction (constructing a polytope FROM a target null-space direction by projecting the n standard basis vectors orthogonal to it) is illustrated by the single worked example (3 dims, project off (1,1,1) → equilateral triangle) and then generalized by analogy/assertion to n dims and the all-ones vector, without a general proof that the all-ones direction is optimal beyond the informal appeal to symmetry (equal importance/sparsity ⇒ the discarded direction should itself be maximally symmetric).
- **worked_numbers**: 3 equally-important, equally-sparse features in 2 dimensions (W⊥(1,1,1) in the ambient 3-space) → optimal strategy is an equilateral triangle (every pairwise strategy for 3 features in 2-D corresponds to SOME triangle, and every triangle to some strategy — the paper states this is 'not surprising' from the correspondence, rather than deriving the equilateral case as the loss-minimizer from the loss function directly).

### 10. Reducible vs. irreducible multi-dimensional features: formal definition, and the separability / ϵ-mixture indices
- **statement**: Engels et al. formalize when a candidate multi-dimensional feature is a 'true' irreducible unit (like a circular day-of-week representation) versus merely the sum of two independent or non-co-occurring lower-dimensional features (which should be counted separately). A feature is reducible if SOME rotation+translation of it factors as a product distribution (independence) or as a mixture with a lower-dimensional degenerate component.
- **equation_verbatim**: Definition 2 (reducibility). f is reducible into a, b if ∃ orthonormal d_f×d_f matrix R and constant c such that, writing f ↦ Rf + c ≡ (a; b) [Eq. 1], the transformed distribution p(a,b) satisfies EITHER:
(1) Separable: p(a, b) = p(a)p(b)
(2) Mixture: p(a, b) = w p(a) δ(b) + (1 − w) p(a, b), with 0 < w < 1
Definition 3 (Separability Index and ϵ-Mixture Index):
S(f) ≡ min_{rotation defining a,b} I(a; b)   [Eq. 2]
M_ϵ(f) = max_{v∈R^{d_f}, c∈R} P_{t∈T}( |v·f(t) + c| < ϵ sqrt(E[(v·f(t)+c)^2]) )   [Eq. 3]
- **symbols**: f = a candidate d_f-dimensional feature (a function from a subset of the input/token space into R^{d_f}); R = an orthonormal d_f×d_f rotation matrix; c = an additive constant (translation); a = the first k components, b = the remaining d_f−k components of the rotated+translated feature; p(a,b) = the joint probability density of the feature, conditioned on the input subset where f is active; δ(·) = the Dirac delta function (used to express a degenerate/lower-dimensional mixture component); w = the mixture weight, 0<w<1; I(a;b) = the mutual information between a and b; T = the input/token distribution; t = an individual input/token; v ∈ R^{d_f}, c∈R (reused symbol) = the probing direction and offset defining a band "near zero"; E[·] = expectation over t∈T.
- **assumptions**: Reducibility is checked over ALL possible rotations R and translations c — a feature is only called irreducible if NO such transformation makes it separable or a mixture; the mixture case requires the degenerate component to be STRICTLY lower-dimensional (via the Dirac delta on b) and the two components of the mixture to have DISJOINT support (cannot both be active for the same input); in practice exact separability/mixture is relaxed via the two indices, which are continuous relaxations rather than the strict all-or-nothing Definition 2.
- **derivation_steps**: Definitions 2 and 3 are given directly as definitions (not derived from a more primitive axiom), motivated by two worked canonical examples the source gives explicitly: a one-hot encoding along a simplex is an example of a MIXTURE (each category is a disjoint, lower-dimensional support region — e.g. 'breed of dog'), while a (multivariate) normal distribution is an example of a SEPARABLE feature (any multidimensional Gaussian can be rotated to have a diagonal covariance matrix, footnote 2, hence factors into independent marginals) — e.g. latitude/longitude of a location token. The two indices (Eq 2, Eq 3) are stated as softened, continuous relaxations of the strict binary Definition 2, with smaller S(f) meaning 'more separable' and larger M_ϵ(f) meaning 'more of a mixture' — the source states this correspondence but defers the formal justification of why the relaxed indices approximate the strict definitions to Appendix B ('we expand on the intuition ... in Appendix B').
- **worked_numbers**: Worked numeric instance for the empirically-tested 'days of the week' cluster (Figure 2, PCA components 2 and 3 of a real model's hidden states): M_ϵ(f) = 0.4750, S(f) = 0.9506, with the ϵ-band evaluated at ϵ = 0.1 ("M_ϵ(f) is the percent of dot products within ϵ=0.1 of 0").

### 11. Multi-dimensional superposition hypothesis: δ-orthogonal matrices generalizing δ-orthogonal vectors
- **statement**: Engels et al. restate Elhage et al.'s original (one-dimensional) superposition hypothesis, then propose a strictly stronger, multi-dimensional generalization: hidden states are sums of many sparse, low-dimensional, IRREDUCIBLE features embedded via pairwise δ-orthogonal MATRICES (subspaces) rather than pairwise δ-orthogonal vectors (directions).
- **equation_verbatim**: Definition 4 (δ-orthogonal matrices). Two matrices A_1 ∈ R^{d×d1} and A_2 ∈ R^{d×d2} are δ-orthogonal if |x_1 · x_2| ≤ δ for all unit vectors x_1 ∈ colspace(A_1) and x_2 ∈ colspace(A_2).
Hypothesis 1 (One-Dimensional Superposition Hypothesis, paraphrased from Elhage et al. 2022). Hidden states x_{i,l} are the sum of many (≫ d) sparse one-dimensional features f_i and pairwise δ-orthogonal vectors v_i such that x_{i,l}(t) = Σ_i v_i f_i(t). We set f_i(t) to zero when t is outside the domain of f_i.
Hypothesis 2 (Multi-Dimensional Superposition Hypothesis, changes underlined). Hidden states x_{i,l} are the sum of many (≫ d) sparse low-dimensional irreducible features f_i and pairwise δ-orthogonal matrices V_i ∈ R^{d×d_{fi}} such that x_{i,l}(t) = Σ_i V_i f_i(t). We set f_i(t) to zero when t is outside the domain of f_i.
- **symbols**: d = ambient hidden-state dimension; d1,d2 (and d_{fi}) = subspace dimensions of the two matrices being compared / of feature i; x_{i,l} = the hidden state at layer l for token/input i; v_i = the (1-dimensional) direction vector for feature i under Hypothesis 1; V_i = the (d_{fi}-dimensional) subspace-embedding matrix for feature i under Hypothesis 2; f_i = the (scalar, under H1; vector-valued in R^{d_{fi}}, under H2) feature activation function; t = an input/token.
- **assumptions**: Both hypotheses require the number of features to be much larger than the ambient dimension (≫ d) and require the features to be SPARSE; Hypothesis 2 additionally requires each f_i to be IRREDUCIBLE (per Definition 2) — this is the added strictness. The paper explicitly notes: 'since multi-dimensional features can be written as the sums of projections of lower-dimensional features, our new superposition hypothesis is a stricter version of Hypothesis 1' — i.e. H2 ⇒ H1 is claimed but not formally proven in-line (the claim rests on the projection argument being self-evident from the definitions).
- **derivation_steps**: source states both hypotheses directly as definitions/hypotheses, not as derived consequences of a more primitive assumption; the strictness relationship between H1 and H2 is asserted with a one-sentence informal justification (any multi-dim feature can be decomposed into projections onto 1-D directions, so H2's structure can always be rewritten in H1's form, but not vice versa) rather than a formal proof.
- **worked_numbers**: none in this definitional section (worked numbers for the underlying capacity claim appear in Appendix A / Theorem 1, captured separately above).
- **source**: Engels, Michaud, Liao, Gurnee, Tegmark, "Not All Language Model Features Are Linear" (2024)
- **locator**: Section 3.2, "Superposition," page 4 (pdftotext -layout lines ~295–313).

### 12. The Fourier-multiplication algorithm for modular addition, and its closed-form trig-identity step
- **statement**: Nanda et al. reverse-engineer the one-layer transformer trained on modular addition and find it implements a closed-form trigonometric algorithm: embed each token as sines/cosines at a sparse set of key frequencies, use trig angle-addition identities (computed by attention+MLP) to form cos/sin of w_k(a+b), then use a further angle-SUBTRACTION identity (computed by the unembedding) to read off, for each candidate answer c, a term that constructively interferes exactly at the true answer c*=a+b mod P.
- **equation_verbatim**: w_k = 2kπ/P , k ∈ N
cos(w_k(a+b)) = cos(w_k a) cos(w_k b) − sin(w_k a) sin(w_k b)   [angle-addition identity; note: an earlier occurrence of this same identity on page 3 of the source PDF is misprinted as 'cos(w_k a) cos(w_k a)' — i.e. cos(w_k b) is erroneously typeset as a second cos(w_k a) — confirmed as a source-side typo present in BOTH pdftotext -layout and plain extraction at that location, NOT an extraction artifact, since the correct form is printed elsewhere (page 7) in the same document]
sin(w_k(a+b)) = sin(w_k a) cos(w_k b) + cos(w_k a) sin(w_k b)
cos(w_k(a+b−c)) = cos(w_k(a+b)) cos(w_k c) + sin(w_k(a+b)) sin(w_k c)   [Equation (1) in the source, angle-subtraction identity]
- **symbols**: a, b = the two one-hot-encoded input tokens (integers mod P); c = a candidate output logit index, ranging over {0,...,P−1}; c* = a+b mod P, the true answer; P = the modulus (prime); w_k = the k-th frequency used by the model; k = a frequency index.
- **assumptions**: The embedding matrix is assumed (and later empirically verified) to be sparse in the Fourier basis, i.e. only a handful of frequencies w_k carry significant weight (the paper finds exactly 5 'key frequencies' for its mainline model); the model's skip connection around the MLP is empirically found to be insignificant, licensing the approximation Logits(a,b) ≈ W_U W_out MLP(a,b) (used to define the 'neuron-logit map' W_L = W_U W_out); constructive interference at c=c* and destructive interference elsewhere follows from the standard trigonometric identity that summing cos(w_k(a+b−c)) over multiple k values is maximized exactly when a+b≡c mod P (since w_k is a multiple of 2π/P) — the paper states this as the reason the readout works, without a separate multi-frequency interference derivation.
- **derivation_steps**: The four-step algorithm (embed→angle-add→angle-subtract-and-sum→read off) is presented as a CLAIM to be justified empirically (four lines of evidence: periodicity of weights/activations; W_L's low-rank sinusoidal structure; degree-2-polynomial-of-single-frequency approximability of individual neurons; ablation robustness), not derived from a training-dynamics argument — i.e. the paper reverse-engineers and VERIFIES this closed form empirically rather than proving from the loss function that gradient descent must find it. The angle-addition/angle-subtraction identities themselves are standard trigonometric identities, stated (with the one printed typo noted above) rather than re-derived from Euler's formula or similar.
- **worked_numbers**: The 5 key frequencies found for the mainline model: k ∈ {14, 35, 41, 42, 52} (out of frequencies 1..56, since k and P−k give symmetric norms). Table 1 (fitted, per-frequency neuron-to-logit coefficients, only top-2 Fourier terms shown per row): for k=14, cos(w14 c) row fits as 44.6·cos(w14 a)cos(w14 b) − 43.6·sin(w14 a)sin(w14 b) ≈ 44.1·cos(w14(a+b)), fraction-of-variance-explained (FVE) = 93.2%; sin(w14 c) row: 44.1·sin(w14 a)cos(w14 b) + 44.1·cos(w14 a)sin(w14 b) ≈ 44.1·sin(w14(a+b)), FVE=93.5%. Similar rows for k=35 (FVE 96.8%/96.5%), k=41 (97.0%/97.0%), k=42 (96.4%/96.4%), k=52 (97.4%/98.2%). Neuron-level: out of 512 total MLP neurons, 433 (84.6%) have over 85% of their variance explained by a degree-2 polynomial of a single frequency.
- **source**: Nanda, Chan, Lieberum, Smith, Steinhardt, "Progress Measures for Grokking via Mechanistic Interpretability" (ICLR 2023)

### 13. Progress measures for grokking: restricted loss, excluded loss, Gini coefficient, and the three-phase timeline
- **statement**: Given the reverse-engineered Fourier-multiplication algorithm, the authors define two ablation-based scalar 'progress measures' that can be tracked DURING training (unlike final-model-only test accuracy) to reveal that grokking is preceded by two earlier, invisible-to-loss-curves phases: memorization, then circuit formation, then a final cleanup phase where the visible test-accuracy jump occurs.
- **equation_verbatim**: [Both progress measures are defined PROCEDURALLY in prose, not as closed-form equations:] "Restricted loss. ... we perform a 2D DFT on the logits to write them as a linear combination of waves in a and b, and set all terms besides the constant term and the 20 terms corresponding to cos(w_k(a+b)) and sin(w_k(a+b)) for the five key frequencies to 0. We then measure the loss of the ablated network." "Excluded loss. Instead of keeping the important frequencies w_k, we next remove only those key frequencies from the logits but keep the rest." "(1) the Gini coefficient (Hurley & Rickard, 2009) of the norms of the Fourier components of W_E and W_L ... and (2) the ℓ2-norm of the weights during training"
- **symbols**: W_E = embedding matrix; W_L = W_U W_out = the neuron-logit map; the '20 terms' = 4 trig terms (cos, sin of w_k(a+b), plus presumably paired terms — paper states 20 = 4 terms × 5 key frequencies) per the 5 key frequencies, plus a constant term.
- **assumptions**: Restricted loss assumes the meaningful signal is fully captured by the constant term plus the cos/sin(w_k(a+b)) terms at exactly the final model's 5 key frequencies (i.e. it presupposes knowledge of which frequencies end up mattering, obtained from analyzing the FINAL trained model, then applied retroactively to INTERMEDIATE checkpoints); excluded loss is measured on the TRAINING data specifically (not test data), because its purpose is to isolate how much of TRAIN performance comes from the generalizing (Fourier) mechanism vs. memorization — the reasoning given: a memorizing solution should be spread out (not concentrated) in the Fourier domain, so ablating a few key directions should barely hurt it, while a genuinely generalizing solution should be hurt badly by the same ablation.
- **derivation_steps**: Both measures are defined as direct translations of the ablation experiments from Section 4.4 into training-time diagnostics — the paper states this translation explicitly ('We translate the ablations in Section 4.4 into two progress measures') rather than deriving either measure from a stated optimality or information-theoretic criterion. No closed-form equation for either loss is given; both are procedural (DFT → zero out a specified term-set → re-evaluate loss).
- **worked_numbers**: Three-phase timeline (mainline model): Memorization, epochs 0–1.4k (excluded loss and train loss both decline; test loss, restricted loss, and Gini coefficient stay flat/high); Circuit formation, epochs 1.4k–9.4k (excluded loss RISES, sum-of-squared-weights FALLS, restricted loss starts falling, test/train loss stay flat — 'the circuit is formed well before grokking occurs'); Cleanup, epochs 9.4k–14k (excluded loss plateaus, restricted loss keeps dropping, test loss suddenly drops, sum-of-squared-weights sharply drops, Gini coefficient sharply increases). Ablation-validation numbers (Section 4.4): replacing the 433 well-approximated neurons with their degree-2-polynomial fits changes loss from 2.41·10⁻⁷ to 2.48·10⁻⁷ (+3% relative, no accuracy change); restricting neuron activations to only the key-frequency trig terms IMPROVES loss by 77% (to 5.54·10⁻⁸); ablating all 113·113−40 non-key-frequency logit Fourier components IMPROVES loss by 70% (to 7.24·10⁻⁸).
- **source**: Nanda, Chan, Lieberum, Smith, Steinhardt, "Progress Measures for Grokking via Mechanistic Interpretability" (ICLR 2023)
- **locator**: Section 5.1 "Progress Measures" and 5.2 "Phases of Grokking," page 8 (pdftotext -layout lines ~613–658); ablation numbers from Section 4.4, page 7 (lines ~522–541).

### 14. The cross-entropy + weight-decay loss, and why perfect-accuracy classifiers are always incentivized to scale up their logits (Theorem D.1)
- **statement**: Varma et al.'s circuit-efficiency framework starts from the standard weight-decay-regularized cross-entropy objective, and proves the specific lemma that grokking's whole story depends on: once a classifier is perfectly accurate on its training set, cross-entropy loss can ALWAYS be strictly reduced further by uniformly scaling up every logit by any constant c>1 — the pressure that competes against weight decay's pressure to shrink parameters.
- **equation_verbatim**: Overall loss: L(h_θ) = L_{x-ent}(h) + α L_{wd}(h_θ) ,  where L_{wd}(h_θ) = (1/2)(P_{h_θ})^2   [Equation (2)]
Cross-entropy loss (Appendix D restatement): L_{x-ent}(h) = −(1/D) Σ_{(x,y)∈D} log( exp(o_h^y) / Σ_{y′∈Y} exp(o_h^{y′}) )
Theorem D.1. Suppose that the classifier h has perfect accuracy, that is, for any (x,y*)∈D and any y′≠y* we have o_h^{y*} > o_h^{y′}. Then, for any c>1, we have L_{x-ent}(c·h) < L_{x-ent}(h).
Proof (rewritten form): L_{x-ent}(h) = (1/D) Σ_{(x,y*)} log( 1 + Σ_{y′≠y*} exp(o_h^{y′} − o_h^{y*}) )
- **symbols**: θ = the classifier's parameter vector, inducing classifier h_θ; P_{h_θ} := ‖θ‖ = the parameter norm; α = the weight-decay coefficient trading off cross-entropy against parameter-norm penalty; D = training dataset, |D| also denoted D (size); (x,y) = an input-label pair; o_h^y(x) (abbreviated o_h^y) = the logit that classifier h assigns to label y on input x; Y = the label set; c·h = the classifier whose logits are all multiplied by scalar c; y* = the true/correct label for a given x.
- **assumptions**: The classifier already achieves PERFECT training accuracy — i.e. the true-label logit strictly exceeds every other logit for every training example — before the scaling argument applies; weight decay is modeled as (1/2)‖θ‖², i.e. standard L2 (Lq with q=2, in the paper's later generalized notation); the argument is about the CROSS-ENTROPY term alone (in isolation, ignoring L_wd, which is what then creates the competing pressure once both terms are considered together).
- **derivation_steps**: Full proof given: (1) rewrite the softmax cross-entropy sum by dividing numerator and denominator by exp(o_h^{y*}), turning the softmax ratio into 1/(1+Σ_{y′≠y*}exp(o_h^{y′}−o_h^{y*})), so L_{x-ent}(h) = (1/D)Σ log(1+Σ_{y′≠y*} exp(o_h^{y′}−o_h^{y*})); (2) since perfect accuracy gives o_h^{y*} > o_h^{y′} for every wrong y′, the logit GAP (o_h^{y*}−o_h^{y′}) is strictly positive, so for any c>1, c·(gap) > gap, i.e. c(o_h^{y′}−o_h^{y*}) < (o_h^{y′}−o_h^{y*}) (multiplying a negative quantity by c>1 makes it more negative); (3) since exp, log, and finite sums are all monotonically increasing, applying c to every logit strictly decreases every exp(o_h^{y′}−o_h^{y*}) term, hence strictly decreases the sum, hence strictly decreases the log, hence strictly decreases L_{x-ent}(c·h) relative to L_{x-ent}(h). QED (□ in source).
- **worked_numbers**: none (fully symbolic proof; this theorem is used qualitatively to motivate the 'feature benefit vs. weight-decay cost' tension that later grounds the circuit-efficiency argument for grokking).
- **source**: Varma, Shah, Kenton, Kramár, Kumar, "Explaining Grokking Through Circuit Efficiency" (2023)
- **locator**: Equation (2), page 3 (pdftotext -layout line ~135); Theorem D.1 and its full proof, Appendix D, pages 23–24 (pdftotext -layout lines ~1364–1391).

### 15. Circuit efficiency and the weight-decay-optimal allocation of parameter norm across competing circuits (Theorem D.4 / Equation 6)
- **statement**: Varma et al. formalize 'circuit efficiency' (how much parameter norm a circuit needs to produce a given logit magnitude) and prove, for a family of circuits that all produce IDENTICAL training logits (so cross-entropy can't distinguish them), exactly how gradient descent under weight decay allocates weight across them — and that the answer bifurcates sharply depending on a single exponent comparison (κ vs. q), directly explaining why a more-efficient generalizing circuit can come to dominate a less-efficient memorizing circuit.
- **equation_verbatim**: Generalized loss (Equation 6): L(w⃗) = L_{train}(Σ_{i=1}^I w_i C_i) + (α/2) Σ_{i=1}^I (1/κ)(w_i^{1/κ} P_i)^q   [Note: the printed exponent placement around 'α/2 Σ (1/κ)(...)^q' reflects the source's own layout, which visually stacks 1/κ as a coefficient inside the sum before the (w_i^{1/κ}P_i)^q term — transcribed as printed by pdftotext -layout; cross-checked against the un-generalized q=2 special case given in prose just before, L(w⃗)=L_{x-ent}(Σw_iC_i) + (α/2)Σ(w_i^{1/κ}P_i)^2, which lacks the extra 1/κ factor — flagging this as a possible OCR duplication of the 1/κ term rather than asserting which is correct.]
Theorem D.4. Given I circuits C_i and associated L_q parameter norms P_i, assume every circuit produces the same logits on the training dataset (∀i,j,∀(x,_)∈D,∀y′∈Y: o_{C_i}^{y′}(x) = o_{C_j}^{y′}(x)). Then any weight vector w⃗* that minimizes the loss in Equation 6 subject to w_i≥0 satisfies:
1. If κ ≥ q, then w_i* = 0 for all i such that P_i > min_j P_j.
2. If 0 < κ < q, then w_i* ∝ P_i^{−q/(q−κ)}.
- **symbols**: I = number of competing circuits; C_i = the i-th circuit (a normalized building block producing a fixed average logit value); w_i = the learned scalar weight scaling circuit i's contribution; P_i = the L_q parameter norm of the NORMALIZED circuit C_i (efficiency proxy — lower P_i means more efficient); κ = the exponent governing how parameter norm scales with weight (motivated by: in a κ-layer ReLU MLP without biases, scaling ALL parameters by c scales the OUTPUT by c^κ, so achieving output-scale w_i requires parameter-scale w_i^{1/κ}); q = the order of the norm used in weight decay (q=2 is standard L2 weight decay); L_train = any differentiable, boundedly-below training loss (generalizing cross-entropy); B = the lower bound on L_train (B=0 for cross-entropy).
- **assumptions**: All I circuits are normalized to produce the SAME average logit magnitude at weight 1, differing only in the parameter norm P_i required to do so; critically, the theorem's identical-training-logits assumption means L_train is a function ONLY of Σ_i w_i (not of the individual w_i), which is what makes the weight-reallocation argument tractable; κ>0, q>0.
- **derivation_steps**: The paper gives an intuition paragraph plus a proof 'Sketch' (not a full formal proof in the main proof block, though it is completed in the surrounding Appendix D text): (1) since all circuits give identical logits, L_train depends only on Σw_i, so any small increase δw to w_i can be exactly offset by an equal decrease δw to some w_j WITHOUT changing L_train; (2) the corresponding change in L_wd from increasing w_i by δw is (∂L_wd/∂w_i)·δw = ακ(P_i(w_i)^r)^q · δw where r := 1/κ − 1/q = (q−κ)/(qκ) [derived by differentiating (w_i^{1/κ}P_i)^q with respect to w_i]; (3) so an increase δw to w_i can be balanced (to first order) by a decrease of (P_i/P_j)(w_i/w_j)^r · δw to w_j; (4) the sign of r splits into two cases: CASE r≤0 (i.e. κ≥q): for P_j>P_i, optimality requires w_i*≥w_j* (else swap them to reduce loss), and then w_j* must be EXACTLY ZERO — because otherwise shifting weight from the less-efficient circuit j to the more-efficient circuit i strictly decreases L_wd while leaving L_train unchanged (a 'rich get richer' / winner-take-all effect: only the single minimal-P_i circuit gets any weight); CASE r>0 (i.e. 0<κ<q): the same swap argument shows w_j* CANNOT be zero (moving weight the other way would help), so the optimum is an interior balance point where P_j(w_j*)^r = P_i(w_i*)^r for all pairs, which solving gives w_i* ∝ P_i^{−1/r} = P_i^{−q/(q−κ)} — every circuit gets SOME weight, but more efficient (lower-P_i) circuits get proportionally more. The paper's own intuitive gloss for WHY the κ-vs-q comparison matters: L_wd grows as O(w_i^{2/κ}) in this parametrization; when κ>2, L_wd grows sublinearly in w_i so concentrating weight on the single best circuit is cheapest; when κ<2, L_wd grows superlinearly so spreading weight is cheaper — hence the winner-take-all vs. shared-allocation split.
- **worked_numbers**: none numerically instantiated (all results are stated in terms of the free exponents κ and q; the paper elsewhere states standard weight decay corresponds to q=2, but does not fix a numeric κ).
- **source**: Varma, Shah, Kenton, Kramár, Kumar, "Explaining Grokking Through Circuit Efficiency" (2023)
- **locator**: Section 3, prose motivation and the q=2-special-case loss, pages 3–4 (pdftotext -layout lines ~193–210); generalized Equation (6) and Theorem D.4 with sketch, Appendix D.1, pages 24–25 (pdftotext -layout lines ~1478–1547).

### 16. The critical dataset-size threshold D_crit for the memorizing-vs-generalizing efficiency crossover (qualitative)
- **statement**: Building on the efficiency framework, Varma et al. argue (without a closed-form D_crit) that a memorizing circuit's efficiency should degrade roughly monotonically as training-set size D grows (because it must encode more and more idiosyncratic input-output pairs), while a genuinely generalizing circuit's efficiency stays roughly constant in D — so there should exist a critical dataset size D_crit above which the generalizing circuit becomes the more efficient one, predicting grokking should occur precisely when D ≫ D_crit and should NOT occur when D ≪ D_crit.
- **equation_verbatim**: [No closed-form equation for D_crit is given anywhere in the main text; the argument is entirely a monotonicity/crossover argument in prose, e.g.: "we should expect 𝐶gen's efficiency to remain unchanged as 𝐷 increases arbitrarily high ... In contrast, 𝐶mem must change with nearly every new data point, and so we should expect its efficiency to decrease as 𝐷 increases. Thus, when 𝐷 is sufficiently large, we expect 𝐶gen to be more efficient than 𝐶mem."]
- **symbols**: D = training dataset (also its size); D′ = D ∪ {(x,y*)}, the dataset with one additional labeled point appended; h_D, h_{D′} = the weight-decay-optimal classifiers trained on D, D′ respectively; D_crit = the (informally defined) critical dataset size at which C_mem and C_gen become approximately equally efficient.
- **assumptions**: The monotonic-non-increasing-efficiency-in-D argument itself rests on an informal optimality argument (given, not a formal theorem): if h_{D′} were STRICTLY more efficient than h_D, it would need to also achieve at least as good L_x-ent AND lower L_wd on D alone, which would contradict h_D being optimal for D — this argument is stated in prose ('Intuitively, ... if it was, then h_D′ would outperform h_D even on just D') rather than formalized as a lemma/theorem with a proof block; whether adding the new point HURTS the existing classifier's cross-entropy on that point is treated as the pivotal case split (generalizes correctly on the new point ⇒ efficiency likely unchanged; fails to generalize ⇒ efficiency likely decreases) but no formal bound on the SIZE of the decrease is given.
- **derivation_steps**: source states without a closed-form derivation — this is explicitly a qualitative/intuitive argument used to motivate two later EMPIRICAL predictions (ungrokking: shrinking D below D_crit after grokking has already occurred should reverse it via catastrophic forgetting; semi-grokking: D near D_crit should produce partial/unstable generalization), both of which the paper then tests experimentally rather than derives analytically. - **worked_numbers**: none — D_crit is never numerically computed or bounded in closed form anywhere in the accessible text; the paper only gestures at the existence of a crossover, e.g. speculating extremely small datasets ("say, D<5") should trivially favor C_mem.

### 17. (Tangential) Causal inner product: a non-Euclidean notion of "orthogonal" for concept directions
- **statement**: Park, Choe, and Veitch's paper is primarily about formalizing the linear representation hypothesis via counterfactual pairs and is NOT centrally about superposition capacity, interference cost, sparsity tradeoffs, or grokking. Its one point of tangential relevance to Appendix B's geometry discussion is a caution: 'orthogonal' need not mean Euclidean-orthogonal. The paper defines a 'causal inner product' under which causally SEPARABLE concepts (e.g. English⇒French and Male⇒Female) are orthogonal by construction, generalizing the Euclidean inner product implicitly assumed throughout the Elhage/Engels superposition-geometry results.
- **equation_verbatim**: Definition 3.1 (Causal Inner Product). A causal inner product ⟨·,·⟩_C on Γ̄ ≃ R^d is an inner product such that ⟨γ̄_W, γ̄_Z⟩_C = 0, for any pair of causally separable concepts W and Z.
[General form, stated separately:] ⟨γ̄, γ̄′⟩_C = γ̄^T M γ̄′ for some symmetric positive definite matrix M.
- **symbols**: Γ̄ = the d-dimensional real vector space of DIFFERENCES between unembedding representations; γ̄_W, γ̄_Z = the canonical unembedding representations (unit-normalized directions) of concepts W and Z; W, Z = two concepts said to be 'causally separable' if the counterfactual intervention setting W=w does not affect the (well-defined) probability of Z=z, for any w,z; M = a symmetric positive-definite matrix parametrizing a valid causal inner product (any such M works; the paper notes the causal inner product is UNDERDETERMINED by the stated axioms — 'd(d−1)/2 constraints' vs. 'd(d−1)/2+d degrees of freedom' in choosing M).
- **assumptions**: Requires the existence, for any concept W, of d−1 other concepts {Z_i} that are each pairwise causally separable with W and whose representations together form a basis of R^d (needed for the companion unification theorem, not for Definition 3.1 itself); causal separability is itself defined via counterfactual well-definedness of P(Y | W=w, Z=z), a notion developed earlier in the paper (Section 2), not re-derived here.
- **derivation_steps**: Definition 3.1 is stated directly as a definitional requirement (not derived); the paper's own remark that this leaves the causal inner product underdetermined is given as a direct constraint-counting argument (d(d−1)/2 orthogonality constraints among d basis concepts vs. d(d−1)/2+d free parameters in a general symmetric positive-definite M), not as a proof.
- **worked_numbers**: none directly germane to superposition; this item is included ONLY as a geometric caveat for Appendix B, not as load-bearing derivation material.
- **source**: Park, Choe, Veitch, "The Linear Representation Hypothesis and the Geometry of Large Language Models" (2024)
