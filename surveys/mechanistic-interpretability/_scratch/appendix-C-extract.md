### 1. Total Effect (TE) of an intervention
- **statement**: The unit-level total effect of setting x=x (vs. a null/reference x) on outcome y in unit u is the proportional change in y caused by the intervention, with no downstream variable held fixed.
- **equation_verbatim**: TE(set-gender, null; y, u) = (y_{set-gender}(u) - y_{null}(u)) / y_{null}(u) = y_{set-gender}(u)/y_{null}(u) - 1.   (2)

TE(set-gender, null; y) = E_u[ y_{set-gender}(u)/y_{null}(u) - 1 ].   (4)
- **symbols**: u = a unit (an input example, e.g. a sentence); x = the intervention variable (here the do-operation, e.g. set-gender vs null); y_x(u) = the value y attains in unit u under intervention do(x=x); TE(x=x, x'=null; y, u) = unit-level total effect; E_u[...] = expectation over the population of units (Eq. 4 is the population-averaged TE).
- **assumptions**: y_null(u) != 0 for all u in the population (proportional-difference form is undefined otherwise, motivating the paper's use of a ratio-based bias metric rather than a raw difference). The do-operation must be well defined on the input distribution (e.g. gender-swappable profession words must exist in the prompt).
- **derivation_steps**: Source gives the unit-level definition directly from the definition of proportional difference between y under the two do-operations (no further derivation supplied); the population average (Eq 4) is obtained by taking E_u of the unit-level quantity, stated without additional derivation.
- **worked_numbers**: Worked example (Fig. 4): u = 'The nurse said that ___'. p([he]|u) ≈ 3.1%, p([she]|u) ≈ 22.4%, so y_null(u) = 3.1/22.4 ≈ 0.14. After set-gender (nurse→man): p([he]) ≈ 31.5%, p([she]) ≈ 2.4%, so y_set-gender(u) = 31.5/2.4 ≈ 13.1. TE = 13.1/0.14 − 1 ≈ 92.6.
- **source**: vig-causal-mediation-2020.pdf
- **locator**: §3.2.1, p.8, Eqs. (2) and (4); worked example Fig. 4, p.8

### 2. Natural Direct Effect (NDE) and Natural Indirect Effect (NIE)
- **statement**: The direct effect of x on y is the change in y from applying the intervention while holding a hypothesized mediator z fixed at its pre-intervention (null) value — i.e. the part of the total effect that does NOT flow through z. The indirect effect is the change in y from setting z to the value it would take under the intervention while holding the rest of the input at its original (null) value — i.e. the part of the effect that flows exclusively through z.
- **equation_verbatim**: NDE(set-gender, null; y) = E_u[ y_{set-gender, z_null(u)}(u) / y_null(u) − 1 ].   (5)

NIE(set-gender, null; y) = E_u[ y_{null, z_set-gender(u)}(u) / y_null(u) − 1 ].   (6)
- **symbols**: z = the mediator (a neuron, a layer, an attention head, or an attention weight); z_null(u) = the value the mediator takes in unit u under the null (no intervention) condition; z_set-gender(u) = the value the mediator takes under the set-gender intervention; y_{x, z_v(u)}(u) = the value y attains when the input receives do-operation x AND the mediator is additionally set (do'd) to the value it would have taken in unit u under condition v.
- **assumptions**: The mediator z is well-defined as an intermediate model component whose value can be independently set (via activation patching) regardless of the value of x; the natural-effects decomposition (Pearl 2001) assumes no interaction between the intervention and the mediator's baseline confounders (standard mediation-analysis 'no mediator-outcome confounding' condition is implicitly assumed by treating y_{x,z} as identified from model interventions rather than observational data).
- **derivation_steps**: Source states the NDE/NIE definitions directly (following Pearl 2001's natural direct/indirect effect definitions), instantiated for the gender-bias use case; no further algebraic derivation from TE is given in this section, though the paper states as a finding (not proven, but empirically observed and called 'decomposable') that TE approximates NDE + NIE ('Gender bias is decomposable: The total gender bias effect approximates the sum of the direct and indirect effect, a surprising result given the non-linear nature of the model').
- **worked_numbers**: None given as a single numeric instance for NDE/NIE at this point in the text (Figure 3 illustrates schematically); quantitative NDE/NIE values appear later per-model in bar charts (Fig. 8) rather than as printed numbers in the body text.
- **source**: vig-causal-mediation-2020.pdf
- **locator**: §3.2.2, p.8, Eqs. (5) and (6); 'decomposable' finding stated in Introduction, p.2 (bullet list) and §5.4

### 3. Concurrent-intervention identity: TE = NIE-all when the mediator set covers everything
- **statement**: If the mediator set S is chosen to be literally every unit that could carry the effect (e.g. all neurons), then by construction the indirect effect of intervening on all of S equals the total effect — there is no room left for a direct effect not mediated by S.
- **equation_verbatim**: By definition, concurrent intervention on all neurons entails TE = NIE-all, since then y_{set-gender}(u) = y_{null,z_set-gender(u)}(u).
- **symbols**: NIE-all = the indirect effect measured by patching all mediators (e.g. all neurons) concurrently, as opposed to NIE-sum = the sum of the indirect effects measured by patching each mediator independently and adding the results.
- **assumptions**: The mediator set literally exhausts every causal pathway between x and y (true for 'all neurons' in a feedforward computation, since neurons together constitute the entire representation available to downstream computation).
- **derivation_steps**: Source states this as an immediate consequence of the NIE definition (Eq. 6) when z_set-gender(u) covers the entire representation, so that null,z_set-gender(u) coincides with the fully-intervened condition set-gender; given without further formal proof beyond this one-sentence argument.
- **worked_numbers**: none
- **source**: vig-causal-mediation-2020.pdf
- **locator**: §5.3 'Neurons' paragraph, p.16

### 4. Synergism / interaction term: NIE-sum vs. NIE-all diverge for a jointly-patched SET of components
- **statement**: Summing the indirect effects of patching each component independently (NIE-sum) does not equal the indirect effect of patching the whole set concurrently (NIE-all); the gap is the interaction/synergy term. For attention heads the gap is small (heads act near-independently); for neurons the gap is large (NIE-sum << NIE-all, i.e. neurons combine synergistically / super-additively to compound the effect beyond what any linear sum of individual patches would predict).
- **equation_verbatim**: Figure 8 compares indirect effects of concurrent intervention on all heads (NIE-all) to summing the effects of independent interventions (NIE-sum). [...] Notably, the sum of independent indirect effects (NIE-sum) is much smaller than the concurrent intervention (NIE-all) [for neurons]. Thus, neurons combine synergistically to compound independent effects.
- **symbols**: NIE-all = NIE of patching the entire component set concurrently; NIE-sum = sum over components c of NIE(patch c alone).
- **assumptions**: Implicitly assumes the mediator components (heads, or neurons) are non-overlapping causal pathways so that a sum-of-singles baseline is a meaningful null hypothesis for 'no interaction'.
- **derivation_steps**: Source states without a closed-form algebraic decomposition (no ANOVA-style formula for the interaction term is given anywhere in this paper); the finding is established purely empirically by comparing the two measured quantities (NIE-all vs. NIE-sum) across models/datasets.
- **worked_numbers**: Attention heads: 'maximum relative distance from NIE-all between 0.7% and 11.3%' across GPT2 variants/datasets — near-independent/complementary. Neurons: NIE-sum is 'much smaller' than NIE-all (no single scalar given in main text; comparison is via Fig. 8 bar chart across GPT2-distil/small/medium/large/xl on Winobias).
- **source**: vig-causal-mediation-2020.pdf
- **locator**: §5.3 'Synergism' (Attention subsection and Neurons subsection), p.15-16; Figure 8, p.16

### 5. Formal causal model, intervention, and interventional (foundations for the do-operator on an activation)
- **statement**: A deterministic causal model assigns each variable a value via a mechanism function of all variables. A hard intervention (the classical do-operator) replaces a variable's mechanism with a constant function, fixing it regardless of its would-be parents. Soft interventions and interventionals generalize this to functional (input-dependent) replacements — the general formal object underlying 'patch this activation with this other value.'
- **equation_verbatim**: Definition 5: A (deterministic) causal model is a pair M = (Sigma, {F_X}_{X in V}), such that Sigma is a signature and {F_X}_{X in V} is a set of mechanisms, with F_X: Val_V -> Val_X assigning a value to X as a function of the values of all the variables, including X itself.

Definition 9 (Intervention): Define a hard intervention to be a partial setting i in Val_I for finite I subset V. Given a model M with signature Sigma, define M_i to be just like M, except that we replace F_X with the constant function v -> Proj_X(i) for each X in I.

Definition 10 (Soft Intervention): a family of functions {I_X}_{X in X} where I_X: Val_V -> Val_X for each X in X. Given M, define M_I to be just like M except we replace each F_X with I_X.

Definition 11 (Interventional): a family of functions I = {I_X}_{X in X} where I_X: Soft_X -> Soft_X for each X in X. Define M_I to be just like M, except that we replace each function F_X with the function I_X<F_X>.
- **symbols**: V = set of endogenous variables; Val_X = the range of variable X; Sigma = (V,Val) a signature; F_X = the mechanism (structural equation) for X, a function of the total setting of V; M_i / M_I = the model M with the relevant variables' mechanisms replaced; Proj_X(i) = restriction/projection of the partial setting i onto variable X; Hard_X = Val_X (set of all hard interventions on X); Soft_X = set of all soft interventions on X; Func_X = set of interventionals on X.
- **assumptions**: The signature Sigma is fixed in advance (variables and their possible values known); for the model to have a unique solution, M is typically assumed acyclic (Solve(M) is then a singleton); interventions are exogenous to the mechanism they replace (post-intervention, the intervened variable no longer depends on its former parents).
- **derivation_steps**: Source builds these as a strict definitional hierarchy: hard intervention (Def 9, classical Pearl do-operator) is generalized to soft intervention (Def 10, replace mechanism with an arbitrary function of the full state rather than a constant) which is further generalized to interventional (Def 11, the replacement function may itself depend on the pre-intervention mechanism F_X, not just on V). Each definition is stated formally with no numeric derivation, since these are foundational axioms rather than derived results.
- **worked_numbers**: none (purely formal/symbolic section)
- **source**: geiger-causal-abstraction-2025.pdf
- **locator**: §2.2, Definitions 5, 9, 10, 11, pp.6-8 (Def 5 p.6; Defs 9-11 p.8)

### 6. Interchange Intervention — the formal do-operator implemented by an activation-patching experiment
- **statement**: An interchange intervention on a causal model with input and output variables fixes a target set of variables to the values they would have taken had the model instead been run on a different ('source') input — this is precisely the formal object that an activation-patching experiment estimates: run the model on a base/reference input, but force some internal variables to the values they take on a counterfactual/source input.
- **equation_verbatim**: Definition 44 (Interchange Interventions): Let M be a causal model with input variables X_In ⊆ V. Furthermore, consider source inputs s_1, ..., s_k ∈ Val_{X_In} and disjoint sets of target variables X_1, ..., X_k ⊆ V \ X_In. Define the hard intervention

IntInv(M, ⟨s_1,...,s_k⟩, ⟨X_1,...,X_k⟩) := ∪_{1≤j≤k} Proj_{X_j}(Solve(M_{s_j})).
- **symbols**: M = a causal model with input variables X_In; s_1,...,s_k = k distinct 'source' inputs (each a total setting of X_In); X_1,...,X_k = k pairwise-disjoint sets of 'target' variables in V (the variables to be patched, drawn from j-th source); Solve(M_{s_j}) = the (unique, for acyclic M) total setting solving the model when its inputs are fixed to s_j; Proj_{X_j}(·) = restriction of that solution to the target variables X_j; the union over j builds one combined hard intervention (per Def. 9) that, when applied to M run on a base input, forces each X_j to the value it took under source s_j.
- **assumptions**: M must have input and output variables (acyclic, so Solve(M) is a well-defined singleton for every total input setting); the target sets X_1,...,X_k must be pairwise disjoint (so the resulting hard intervention over their union is well-defined without conflicting assignments); each s_j ranges only over X_In (interchange interventions are defined by varying the input, not by directly injecting an arbitrary internal value).
- **derivation_steps**: Source defines this directly by composing two already-defined operations: (i) solve the model M under a substituted input s_j (Solve(M_{s_j})), (ii) project that solution onto the chosen target variables X_j, then (iii) take the union of these per-target hard-interventions across j=1..k, which by Def. 9 constitutes a single valid hard intervention on M. No further derivation given — this is the base-case definition, later generalized (in the same section) to Recursive Interchange Interventions (Def. 45, composing interchange interventions of increasing depth) and Distributed Interchange Interventions (Def. 46, patching a rotated/bijectively-translated basis rather than the raw variables — directly relevant to patching distributed/superposed features).
- **worked_numbers**: none in this definition (a worked numeric example for the related notion of approximate transformation appears elsewhere, Example 5 in §2.4, computing a degree-of-approximation value of 4.5 for a toy addition-abstraction example — not this definition itself).
- **source**: geiger-causal-abstraction-2025.pdf
- **locator**: §2.5, Definition 44, p.21 (immediately followed by Def. 45 Recursive Interchange Interventions and Def. 46 Distributed Interchange Interventions, same page/p.22)

### 7. Distributed Interchange Interventions — patching a rotated/distributed feature basis
- **statement**: Generalizes interchange interventions to target variables that are distributed across (i.e. linear/nonlinear combinations of) multiple raw causal variables: translate into a new variable space via a bijection, perform an ordinary interchange intervention there, then translate back. This is the formal grounding for patching a learned direction or a transcoder feature rather than a raw neuron/head.
- **equation_verbatim**: Definition 46 (Distributed Interchange Interventions): Let M be a causal model with input variables X_In ⊆ V and let tau: Val_V -> Val_{V*} be a bijective translation that preserves inputs. For source inputs s_1,...,s_k in Val_{X_In}, and disjoint target variables X*_1,...,X*_k ⊆ V* \ X_In, we define DistIntInv(M, tau, ⟨s_1,...,s_k⟩, ⟨X*_1,...,X*_k⟩) to be an interventional on all variables V that replaces each mechanism for X with the function v -> Proj_X( tau^{-1}( Solve( tau(M)_{IntInv(M,⟨s_1,...,s_k⟩,⟨X*_1,...,X*_k⟩)} ) ) ).
- **symbols**: tau = a bijective translation from the original variable space V to a new variable space V* (an invertible change of basis, e.g. a learned rotation matrix); tau(M) = the causal model M re-expressed in the translated variable space; X*_1,...,X*_k = target variables defined in the translated (V*) space; IntInv(...) = the ordinary interchange intervention (Def. 44) performed inside the translated space; tau^{-1} = the inverse translation back to the original space V.
- **assumptions**: tau must be bijective and must preserve the input variables (so the translated model still has a well-defined input-output map); the paper notes (Remark, in the running text) that if the linear representation hypothesis holds, a rotation matrix suffices as tau — otherwise nonlinear bijective translations may be needed to expose modular features not linearly accessible.
- **derivation_steps**: Source builds this directly by composing three already-defined operations — translate (tau), interchange-intervene in the new basis (Def. 44), translate back (tau^{-1}) — and states the resulting interventional in closed form; no numeric derivation, this is a definitional composition.
- **worked_numbers**: none in this definition
- **source**: geiger-causal-abstraction-2025.pdf
- **locator**: §2.5, Definition 46, p.22 (immediately following Definition 44/45)

### 8. What a patching experiment estimates: the AUE/ATE hypothesis-testing framework
- **statement**: A patching hypothesis is formalized as a tuple (graph, dissimilarity metric, an important-node/path set, an input-pair distribution). The quantity a patching experiment actually estimates is the Average Unexplained Effect (AUE) of that hypothesis — the expected dissimilarity between the true model output and the output of the model with the hypothesized-unimportant components patched to a counterfactual input. AUE is compared against the Average Total Effect (ATE, the AUE of the trivial 'no paths are important' hypothesis) to get a 'proportion explained' score.
- **equation_verbatim**: AUE(H) := E_{(x_r,x_c)~D}[ delta(G(x_r), G_H(x_r,x_c)) ]    (1)

ATE(H) := E_{(x_r,x_c)~D}[ delta(G(x_r), G(x_c)) ]

proportion explained = (1 - AUE(H)/ATE(H)) * 100%
- **symbols**: G = the function computed by the network (input x in X, output y in Y); H = (G, delta, S, D) a hypothesis, where delta: Y^2 -> R is a dissimilarity measure (e.g. absolute difference, KL divergence); S = the set of 'important' nodes (G\S = unimportant nodes, patched to counterfactual values); D = a joint distribution over (reference, counterfactual) input pairs (x_r, x_c); G_H(x_r,x_c) = G evaluated on x_r except each unimportant node is replaced with the value it takes when G is evaluated on x_c.
- **assumptions**: x_r is typically drawn from a reference distribution (train/validation set); x_c can be drawn from D_r (resampling), a transformation of x_r (corruption), the dataset mean (mean ablation), or the zero tensor (zero ablation) — different choices give different named experiments; the strictest reading of 'H holds' (delta=0 everywhere on D) is stated to be too stringent for real networks, motivating the graded AUE metric instead.
- **derivation_steps**: Source defines G_H directly (as a graph-surgery operation: replace each unimportant node's value with its value under x_c), then defines AUE as the expectation of delta between the true output G(x_r) and the hypothesis-abstracted output G_H(x_r,x_c) — stated as the formal definition, not derived from a prior result. 'Proportion explained' is then derived by a simple rearrangement: dividing AUE by ATE (the AUE of the null hypothesis that nothing is important) and converting to a percentage; source explicitly notes AUE can exceed ATE, making proportion explained negative.
- **worked_numbers**: In §4 case study (GPT-2 small/XL, 'number' completion task): Table 5 — corrupting heads 5.6 and 6.1 individually reduces P(numeric completion) from a baseline of 88.1% to 82.5% and 83.9% respectively (KL=0.025 and 0.017 vs. baseline), while PATCHING (not corrupting) the same heads has almost no effect (88.0%, 88.3%). §5 greedy-search case study: including only heads 9.1 and 8.8 as important in GPT-2 small recovers 72.8% of the loss; including 8 of 144 heads recovers 98.0%.
- **source**: goldowsky-dill-path-patching-2023.pdf
- **locator**: §2.3, Eq. (1) p.3; §2.5 'Metrics' (proportion explained), p.4-5; Table 5, p.10; Figure 11, p.11

### 9. Treeify(G) and path patching as targeting individual computation PATHS rather than nodes
- **statement**: To patch a single path through the computation graph (rather than a whole node, which would affect all of that node's downstream uses), the graph is 'treeified': every subtree with multiple consumers is copied so each consumer gets its own independent copy of the input. This yields a one-to-one correspondence between paths in the original network and copies of the input node, so a single path can be set to the counterfactual input without touching any other path.
- **equation_verbatim**: G(x) = f1(A(x)) + A(x), where A(x) = f0(x) + x.

After Treeify: G_T(x0, x1, x2, x3) = f1(A(x)) + A(x) = f1(f0(x3) + x2) + f0(x1) + x0.

G_H(x_r, x_c) := G_T(x1, x2, ..., xN) with x_i = { x_r if p_i ∈ P; x_c if p_i ∉ P }
- **symbols**: G = a 2-layer residual network with layer functions f0, f1 and skip connections A, Y; N = the number of distinct paths from input to output after treeification; x0,x1,x2,x3 = the N=4 independently-labeled copies of the input, one per path; p_1,...,p_N = the N paths through G, ordered to match the arguments of G_T; P = the set of paths declared 'important' (kept at the reference/clean value x_r); paths not in P are set to the counterfactual x_c.
- **assumptions**: The network's computation must be expressible as a DAG that can be fully expanded algebraically into a sum-of-paths form (true for residual networks / transformers where skip connections make this decomposition natural, per Veit et al. 2016's observation that residual networks are ensembles of shallow paths); when multiple paths are simultaneously unimportant, the SAME x_c is reused for all of them (an explicit, argued-for default choice in the paper's Appendix A, rather than sampling an independent x_c per path).
- **derivation_steps**: Source derives G_T by (1) fully expanding the algebraic expression for G(x) — substituting A(x)=f0(x)+x into f1(A(x))+A(x) to get f1(f0(x)+x)+f0(x)+x — then (2) relabeling each of the 4 occurrences of x with a unique subscript in arbitrary order, giving G_T(x0,x1,x2,x3) = f1(f0(x3)+x2) + f0(x1) + x0. G_H is then defined by fixing the joint distribution over which x_i get x_r vs x_c according to path membership in P. The paper connects this to Pearl's path-specific effect: if x_r plays the role of the reference x* and x_c the intervention x, and 'unselected paths' correspond to the paper's 'important paths' P, then G_H(x_r,x_c) is exactly Pearl's path-specific effect.
- **worked_numbers**: none in the general derivation (concrete numeric path-patching results are the Table 5 / greedy-search numbers already captured in the AUE/ATE item above)
- **source**: goldowsky-dill-path-patching-2023.pdf
- **locator**: §2.3-2.4, pp.2-4, Figures 1-4; Pearl path-specific-effect correspondence, p.4

### 10. Interaction between components: AND-gate vs OR-gate worked example (denoising vs. noising find different subsets)
- **statement**: When two components A and B jointly gate a downstream behavior via an AND relationship, patching (denoising) either A or B ALONE has no effect — only patching BOTH (or the downstream AND-node C) restores behavior — because the other unpatched component still blocks the gate. The reverse holds for an OR relationship: noising (corrupting) either component alone has no effect, because the other alone still satisfies the gate. This directly demonstrates why the effect of a SET of patches is not the sum of the single-component effects, and why a single patching direction (only noising, or only denoising) can systematically miss circuit components depending on the gate's logical structure.
- **equation_verbatim**: AND circuit: C = A AND B. Denoising (clean -> corrupt patching) either A or B has no effect on the output; only denoising C restores the output [because denoising A still leaves B at the corrupted baseline, and vice versa]. Noising (corrupt -> clean patching) either A or B has an effect, as well as noising C.

OR circuit: C = A OR B. Denoising either A or B has an effect, as well as denoising C. Noising either A or B has no effect on the output; only [noising] C restores the output [because noising A still leaves B at the clean baseline, and vice versa].
- **symbols**: A, B = two upstream model components; C = a downstream component/behavior that is a logical (AND or OR) function of A and B; denoising = clean-run activation patched INTO a corrupted run ('what restores behaviour'); noising = corrupted-run activation patched INTO a clean run ('what breaks behaviour').
- **assumptions**: The two upstream components combine via a genuinely binary logical gate (AND or OR) at C — a simplifying stylization; source explicitly notes real transformer circuits are 'typically much messier,' and that AND/OR structures can arise as serial-dependent components (a later head depending on an earlier one, giving AND-like behavior) or parallel/redundant components (backup attention heads, giving OR-like behavior).
- **derivation_steps**: Source states the result by direct case-walkthrough of the AND and OR truth tables under each patching direction (no algebraic derivation of a general interaction-term formula is given — this is a worked logical/behavioral argument, not an equation). A second, more detailed worked example follows immediately after (the 'Nobel Peace Prize' toy circuit: an attention head L0H0 copies the 'Nobel' embedding to the 'Peace' position, and a neuron L1N42 maps the Nobel+Peace mix to the 'Prize' logit — an AND relationship). There: noising finds ALL THREE components (L0H0, L1N42, and the embeddings) as necessary, while denoising finds only L1N42 as sufficient, because 'if you patch component A in layer N, it has seen clean versions of every component in layers 0 to N-1' — so denoising a late-enough single component can silently subsume an earlier AND-gated dependency.
- **worked_numbers**: No numeric values (illustrative/qualitative example); the paper does note real-world path-patching can quantify this precisely: e.g. 'denoising the paths (1) Nobel->L0H0 and (2) Peace->L1N42 is sufficient [to restore behavior]' while noising every path except three specific ones is required to avoid breaking it — showing the asymmetric minimal sets each patching direction discovers.
- **source**: heimersheim-activation-patching-guide-2024.pdf
- **locator**: §2.4 'Example: AND gate vs OR gate', p.4; §3.1 'Walkthrough a stylized example' (Nobel Peace Prize circuit), p.6

### 11. No minimality: circuits found by patching are sufficient sets, not proven-smallest sets
- **statement**: Standard patching-based circuit discovery only establishes that a discovered component set is SUFFICIENT to reproduce the behavior (patching-in restores it, or patching-out breaks it) — it makes no claim that the set is MINIMAL (no smaller subset would also work). This is stated as an explicit, named limitation of the method class, distinct from a claim about faithfulness.
- **equation_verbatim**: No minimality: Here, and in many parts of the literature, a circuit is treated as a collection of model components that are responsible for a particular model behaviour. We typically make no claims that we have found the smallest such collection of components, we only test that this collection is sufficient.
- **symbols**: (informal prose statement; no equation)
- **assumptions**: n/a — this is a stated methodological limitation rather than a formal result with assumptions.
- **derivation_steps**: source states without derivation (a definitional/methodological claim, not a proven proposition)

### 12. Sufficiency vs. completeness: the AUE/patching metric answers a narrower question than 'is this the whole circuit'
- **statement**: A low-AUE (near-zero unexplained effect) hypothesis proves the chosen component set is SUFFICIENT to mediate the behavior on the tested distribution — it does NOT prove the set is COMPLETE (i.e. that it captures the entirety of how the behavior is implemented, or that no other equally-good smaller/different set exists). A worked conceptual counterexample (majority-vote / saturating-nonlinearity 'voters') shows a proper subset of the true causal components can already achieve AUE=0, so patching-based sufficiency systematically underdetermines completeness.
- **equation_verbatim**: Measuring sufficiency rather than completeness. The AUE metric answers a very specific question: in expectation, how sufficient is your chosen set of paths to mediate changes in output on the given distribution? [...] Therefore, it's important to interpret AUE-based metrics in terms of sufficiency and not completeness.
- **symbols**: AUE(H) = Average Unexplained Effect of hypothesis H, as defined in the AUE/ATE item above.
- **assumptions**: The illustrative example assumes a 'majority vote of a number of identical voters' behavior, or more realistically 'correlated heuristics whose weighted outputs feed into a saturating nonlinearity like sigmoid' — i.e. cases where the true mechanism has built-in redundancy so that many distinct subsets of components independently suffice.
- **derivation_steps**: Source argues by worked conceptual example rather than formal proof: if behavior is majority-vote of N identical voters, ANY subset containing a majority achieves AUE=0 (since the majority already determines the vote outcome regardless of the omitted minority) — so many distinct 'sufficient' hypotheses exist simultaneously, none of which is uniquely the 'complete' explanation, and a hypothesis using all voters is not preferred by AUE despite being (trivially) the complete one, because it is 'less parsimonious.' The saturating-sigmoid variant generalizes this from an exact majority vote to an approximate/graded version.
- **worked_numbers**: none numeric (purely conceptual worked example, no dataset instantiation given)
- **source**: goldowsky-dill-path-patching-2023.pdf
- **locator**: §7.1 'Limitations', 'Measuring sufficiency rather than completeness' paragraph, p.11

### 13. Graded Faithfulness via Approximate Transformation — the general formal faithfulness metric
- **statement**: Faithfulness of a high-level (interpretable) causal model as an explanation of a low-level (neural) one is formalized, not as a binary yes/no, but as a graded 'approximate transformation' score: the expected similarity, under some distribution over interventions, between the low-level model's outcome (translated into the high-level variable space) and the high-level model's outcome under the corresponding intervention. Common interpretability metrics (interchange-intervention accuracy, logit/probability difference, KL-divergence, and causal-scrubbing's 'proportion of performance maintained') are all shown to be special cases of this one general formula.
- **equation_verbatim**: Definition 41 (Approximate Transformation): [...] the metric that quantifies the degree to which M* is an approximate transformation of M in a single number:

S_{I~P}[ Sim( tau(Solve(M_I)), Solve(M*_{omega(I)}) ) ]    (6)

This metric is a graded version of Equation 3 in the definition of exact transformation. If this number is above a particular cutoff eta, we can say that M* is an eta-approximate abstraction of M.
- **symbols**: M = the low-level (e.g. neural network) causal model; M* = the high-level (e.g. interpretable/symbolic) causal model; tau: Val_V -> Val_{V*} = a map from low-level total settings to high-level total settings; omega: Psi -> Psi* = a map from low-level interventionals to high-level interventionals (order-preserving, surjective partial function); Sim = a distance/similarity function between two total settings of M*; P = a probability distribution over interventionals I (drawn from Domain(omega)) describing which interventions are 'expected' to be tested; S = a real-valued summary statistic (e.g. expectation) of the random variable Sim(tau(Solve(M_I)), Solve(M*_{omega(I)})); eta = a chosen cutoff threshold for calling M* an 'eta-approximate abstraction' of M.
- **assumptions**: M and M* must have input and output variables so that Solve(·) is well-defined for a given intervention; tau and omega must be surjective partial functions with omega order-preserving with respect to the intervention algebras (Psi,∘) and (Psi*,∘); the choice of Sim, P, and S are explicitly left open/adjustable to the use case — the paper argues this flexibility is what lets one graded formula subsume many published metrics.
- **derivation_steps**: Source states this is 'a graded version of Equation 3 in the definition of exact transformation' — i.e. exact transformation requires the two sides to be EQUAL for every intervention in P's support; approximate transformation instead averages a similarity score over that distribution, turning a binary all-or-nothing definition into a continuous one. §3.2 then instantiates this general formula: interchange intervention accuracy, probability/logit difference, and KL-divergence are stated to all be recoverable as specific choices of (Sim, P, S); separately, §3.5 shows Chan et al.'s (causal scrubbing) proportion-of-performance-maintained faithfulness metric is 'equivalent to the approximate transformation metric ... with P as a random distribution over inputs and interventionals from Scrub, Sim as a function outputting 1 only if the low-level and high-level outputs are equal, and S as expected value' — none of these equivalences are proven in full symbolic detail in the excerpted text; they are asserted as direct instantiations.
- **worked_numbers**: A related worked example (Example 5, for the general 'approximate transformation' concept, not faithfulness specifically) computes a concrete degree-of-approximation value of 4.5 for a toy model that sums two integers vs. a model that only sums multiples of 10 (E[|(x1+x2)%10 − (x1%10+x2%10)|] = 4.5).
- **source**: geiger-causal-abstraction-2025.pdf
- **locator**: §2.4 Definition 41 and Example 5, p.20; §3.2 'Graded Faithfulness via Approximate Abstraction', pp.31-32; causal-scrubbing equivalence, p.41 (§3.5 area, near line 2357 of extracted text)

### 14. Direct, Indirect, and Total Effect as a structural-causal-model decomposition of a transformer's residual stream (formal, with derivation)
- **statement**: Treating a transformer as a structural causal model (inputs = exogenous variables, activations/outputs = endogenous variables computed by deterministic mechanisms), the Total Effect of ablating a component is the change in output logits allowing the ablation to propagate through the WHOLE downstream network; the Direct Effect is the change in output logits from the ablation ALONE, holding every other (downstream) component fixed at its original value; the Indirect Effect is the complementary piece that flows only through the other, non-ablated downstream components. Because the residual stream is additive, the direct effect of a layer's output on the logits can be computed in closed form via the model's own unembedding matrix (the 'logit lens'), which is exactly what makes 'ablation-based importance' (total effect) and 'unembedding-based importance' (direct effect) two DIFFERENT quantities that can disagree — this disagreement IS self-repair.
- **equation_verbatim**: Definition 3.2 (Total effect): TE(z -> z', Y, u) = Y(u|do(Z=z')) - Y(u|do(Z=z)).   (14)

Definition 3.3 (Direct effect): DE(z -> z', Y, u) = Y(u|do(Z=z', M=m(u))) - Y(u|do(Z=z)).   (19)  [reduces, when z=z(u), to: DE(z->z',Y,u) = Y(u|do(Z=z',M=m(u))) - Y(u).   (20)]

Definition 3.4 (Indirect effect): IE(z -> z', Y, u) = Y(u|do(Z=z, M=m~)) - Y(u|do(Z=z));  m~ = m(x'_{<=t}).   (29)

Unrolled residual stream: z_t^L(x_{<=t}) = sum_{l=1}^{L} m_t^l(x_{<=t}) + a_t^L(x_{<=t}).   (21)

Unembedding: pi_t(x_{<=t}) = RMSNorm(z_t^L) W_U = (1/sigma(z_t^L)) sum_{l=1}^{L} [m_t^l(x_{<=t}) + a_t^l(x_{<=t})] G W_U.   (24)

DE(a_t^l -> a~_t^l, pi_t, u) = [ (a~_t^l - a_t^l(x_{<=t})) / sigma(z_t^L) ] G W_U   (27)  =  u(a~_t^l)_i - u(a_t^l(x_{<=t}))_i.   (28)
- **symbols**: Z = an intervened variable (e.g. attention layer output A_t^l); Y = an outcome variable (e.g. centred logit pi-hat_t of the max-likelihood token); z, z' = pre- and post-intervention values of Z; u = the context (input prompt x_{<=t}); M = V\{Z,Y}, all other variables (held fixed at their context-u value m(u) for the direct effect); m~ = the value M takes under a DIFFERENT context x'_{<=t} (used for indirect effect); a_t^l, m_t^l = attention-layer and MLP-layer outputs at position t, layer l; z_t^L = final residual-stream activation; L = final layer index; sigma(z) = RMSNorm scale factor (=sqrt of mean-square of z); G = learned RMSNorm gain (diagonal matrix); W_U = unembedding matrix; u(·) = the model's unembedding function (logit lens); do(Z=z') = the do-operator, replacing Z's mechanism with a constant z' regardless of its parents.
- **assumptions**: The RMSNorm scale factor sigma(z_t^L) must be held FIXED at its natural (un-ablated) value for the direct-effect-via-unembedding equivalence to be exactly linear (this is an approximation when sigma itself changes post-ablation — the paper notes this discrepancy is 'typically negligible' for well-chosen resample-ablation sources, and exactly zero for zero-ablation); causal masking (t1<t2 => no dependence of position t1 on t2) is assumed for the autoregressive-transformer instantiation; the residual stream is assumed genuinely additive across layers (standard pre-LN transformer architecture).
- **derivation_steps**: Source explicitly derives Eq. (28) from Eq. (25) in numbered steps: (25) direct-effect definition instantiated for a_t^l; (26) substitute the unrolled-residual-stream sum (Eq. 21/24) for both the ablated and un-ablated logit terms, noting all OTHER layer outputs (j != l) are identical in both terms and cancel; (27) after cancellation, only the ablated layer's own output survives, divided by the (held-fixed) RMSNorm scale and multiplied by GW_U; (28) recognize this as exactly u(a~_t^l) - u(a_t^l), i.e. the difference of the model's own logit-lens unembeddings of the ablated and clean activations. The paper states this shows 'the only difference (up to centring) between the direct effect ... and Delta_unembed,l is the inclusion of the impact of the ablation on the maximum-likelihood token,' which is negligible/zero under the stated assumptions — so Delta_unembed IS (up to that correction) the direct effect, while separately (Eqs 14-18, also derived stepwise) Delta_ablate IS exactly the total effect.
- **worked_numbers**: None as a single numeric instance in this derivation (this is the closed-form symbolic derivation); quantitative TE/DE/compensation numbers appear in the companion 'compensatory effect' item below.
- **source**: mcgrath-hydra-effect-2023.pdf
- **locator**: §3.1 'Direct, indirect, and total effects', Definitions 3.2-3.4 and Eqs. (14)-(29), pp.9-11; unrolled-residual-stream derivation Eqs. (21)-(28), pp.10-11

### 15. Toy model of Hydra effect vs. Erasure, and the formal Compensatory Effect (CE) quantifying self-repair
- **statement**: Two minimal mechanisms both make the total effect of an intervention vanish while the direct effect does not: 'Erasure' (a downstream component literally cancels the intervened value, y=0 regardless of context) and 'Self-repair/Hydra' (a downstream component recomputes and re-injects the ORIGINAL context-dependent value that the ablated component would have contributed). The paper then defines a Compensatory Effect that sums, layer by layer, how much every downstream component's OWN direct effect changed as a result of the ablation — this is the formal, additive quantification of 'how much of the ablated component's lost direct effect got made back up by everything downstream,' i.e. exactly why total-effect (ablation-based) importance can be much smaller than direct-effect (unembedding-based) importance would suggest.
- **equation_verbatim**: y = x(u) + f(x,u).   (30)

f(x,u) = -x   (Erasure)   (31)

f(x,u) = { 0 if x=u ; u otherwise }   (Self-repair)   (32)

CE(a~^m, u) = sum_{l=m+1}^{L} Delta_DE(a^l, u, a~^m)  +  sum_{l=m}^{L} Delta_DE(m^l, u, a~^m).   (33)

Delta_DE(z^l, u, z~^m) = DE_ablated(z^l, u, z~^m) - DE(z^l, u),   where DE_ablated(z^l,u,z~^m) = Delta_unembed^{k,l}   (34)
- **symbols**: x(u)=u, y = endogenous variables of a minimal 2-variable toy SCM with exogenous variable u; f(x,u) = the downstream 'repair' function; a~^m = the resample-ablated value substituted at layer m; CE(a~^m,u) = total compensatory effect summed over all downstream attention layers (from m+1) and MLP layers (from m, since MLPs follow attention within a layer) in context u; Delta_DE(z^l,u,z~^m) = the CHANGE in direct effect of downstream component z at layer l, comparing the ablated-at-m network to the intact network; DE_ablated(z^l,u,z~^m) = the direct effect of layer l measured IN the ablated (at layer m) network, which by the earlier item equals Delta_unembed at layer l in the ablated run (superscript k here denotes the ablation layer, i.e. k=m).
- **assumptions**: The toy model (Eqs 30-32) is an explicitly 'stylised' minimal illustration, not a claim about the actual mechanism; the CE decomposition (Eq 33) assumes direct effects are additive across layers (holds under the same RMSNorm-fixed / additive-residual-stream assumptions as the DE derivation above); computed empirically over 1,209 contexts from the Counterfact dataset, ablating attention and MLP layers individually across all layers.
- **derivation_steps**: Source derives the toy model by posing the design question directly: 'If we intervene to set x=0, what functions f will ensure that the total effect TE(x,y) is zero?' and exhibits two solutions (Erasure sets f=-x unconditionally; Self-repair sets f to reconstruct the ORIGINAL u whenever x has been perturbed away from u) — stated as two illustrative solutions, not derived as the unique or exhaustive set. The CE formula (Eq. 33) is defined directly as a sum of the Eq.-34 per-layer direct-effect CHANGES, itself grounded in the earlier DE=Delta_unembed correspondence; no further symbolic simplification of Eq. 33 is given, it is evaluated empirically.
- **worked_numbers**: Layer 23 (of 32, Chinchilla-like model) is the highest-correlation layer: compensation explains 92% of the variance in downstream Delta_DE. A linear regression of compensatory effect on direct effect has SLOPE < 1 at all layers past layer 13 (i.e. compensation is real but incomplete — never fully restores the ablated direct effect). Aggregate finding (Conclusion, §6): at middle layers (where the effect is strongest) the Hydra effect and the reduction in late-MLP erasure COLLECTIVELY restore approximately 70% of the reduction in token logits caused by the ablation.
- **source**: mcgrath-hydra-effect-2023.pdf
- **locator**: §3.3 'Toy model and motif for the Hydra effect', Eqs. (30)-(32), p.12; §4.1 'Methodology', Eqs. (33)-(34), p.12; §4.2 'Results' (92% variance, slope<1 past layer 13), p.13; §6 'Conclusion' (70% restoration figure), p.15

### 16. Self-repair, formally defined per attention head (Rushing 2024) — a directly measurable, per-token quantity
- **statement**: Self-repair is defined per-component as the discrepancy between the OBSERVED change in the correct-token logit after ablating a component, and the change PREDICTED by that component's direct effect alone: if downstream components did nothing special, ablating a head with direct effect DE_head should change the logit by exactly -DE_head (approximately, under resample ablation where the new direct effect is near zero); any additional discrepancy beyond that is attributed to downstream compensation, i.e. self-repair.
- **equation_verbatim**: self-repair = Delta_logit - Delta_DE_head.   (1)
- **symbols**: Delta_logit = logit_ablated - logit_clean, the observed change in the logit of the correct next token after resample-ablating the head; Delta_DE_head = the change in the head's OWN direct effect due to the ablation (approx. equal to -DE_head under resample ablation, since the new/ablated direct effect is near zero for an arbitrary resampled activation); DE_head = the head's direct effect on the correct-token logit in the clean/un-ablated run.
- **assumptions**: Resample ablation is used (replacing the head's output with its value from a DIFFERENT, unrelated pretraining-distribution prompt), which is why Delta_DE_head ≈ -DE_head is a good approximation (the new direct effect is 'near zero' for most heads under this ablation scheme — this approximation would not hold for zero- or mean-ablation in general); the definition explicitly allows self-repair to be negative, but the paper argues NEGATIVE self-repair with a POSITIVE original direct effect should be interpreted as 'downstream breakage,' not self-repair — i.e. Eq. (1) alone does not distinguish the two phenomena, interpretation requires checking the sign of the original DE_head too.
- **derivation_steps**: Source derives the operational form directly from the definition of Delta_logit and the direct-effect definition (following Vig et al. 2020's causal-mediation-analysis / activation-patching setup), reasoning: 'If the output of an attention head has no indirect downstream effects ... then Delta_logit would equal Delta_DE_head. However, in practice these are rarely equal, and often |Delta_DE_head| > |Delta_logit| ... caused by later components changing their direct effects in a way that compensates for the resample ablated component' — this discrepancy IS defined as self-repair (Eq. 1), given as a definition rather than derived from a deeper first-principles model.
- **worked_numbers**: Worked example: 'consider an attention head with a direct effect of 0.5 on the correct output token: if this head is ablated and the logit difference between the original and ablated model is only -0.2 logits, we can approximate the self-repair as -0.2 - (-0.5) = 0.3.' Empirical: on Pythia-1B / The Pile (1M tokens), many later-layer heads show self-repair; hand-picked examples (Pythia-410M, L22H11, L20H6, L23H1, L20H14 over 5000 tokens) show large token-level NOISE in self-repair even where the AVERAGE is clearly self-repairing; a specific falsified hypothesis is quoted: 'the direct effects of all late layer heads are self-repaired by 70%' is immediately falsified by the per-head data (contrast with McGrath et al.'s aggregate ~70% figure at middle layers, which the paper argues does not hold as a uniform per-head rule).
- **source**: rushing-self-repair-2024.pdf
- **locator**: §2.1 'Defining Self-Repair', Eq. (1), p.2-3; worked numeric example p.3; §2.3 'Self-Repair is Noisy' (70% hypothesis falsified), p.3

### 17. LayerNorm/RMSNorm as an additional, 'passive' self-repair mechanism (with derived equation)
- **statement**: Even when NO attention head or MLP compensates for an ablation, the final normalization layer (LayerNorm/RMSNorm) alone can produce apparent self-repair, because ablating a component changes the residual stream's NORM (and hence the normalization scale factor), which rescales ALL existing logits — including the correct one — often in the direction that partially restores it. This is derived as a closed-form decomposition of the post-ablation logit change into a 'rescale the old logits' term and a 'rescale the head's own direct-effect change' term.
- **equation_verbatim**: Delta_logit = (S/S' - 1) * logit_clean  +  (S/S') * (Delta_DE_Head)
[first term labeled: 'LayerNorm on existing logits'; second term labeled: 'LayerNorm on Delta_DE_Head'; S, S' = LayerNorm/RMSNorm scaling factors pre- and post-ablation respectively]
- **symbols**: S = the LayerNorm/RMSNorm scaling factor (proportional to the residual stream's norm) BEFORE ablation; S' = the same scaling factor AFTER ablation; logit_clean = the original (pre-ablation) logit of the correct next token; Delta_DE_Head = the change in the ablated head's own direct effect (as in the self-repair definition above).
- **assumptions**: LayerNorm/RMSNorm is 'folded in' to the adjacent linear layers' weights as in Elhage et al. 2021, isolating the purely nonlinear part of the operation (division by the scale factor S) as the only nonlinear residual-stream operation being analyzed; the derivation is stated to apply identically to RMSNorm (used in LLaMA-style models) as to classic LayerNorm; self-repair via this mechanism specifically requires S > S' (ablation DECREASES the residual-stream norm), which the paper argues is typically the case because 'ablations decrease the norm of the residual stream' when the ablated head's output is correlated with (aligned with) the rest of the residual stream, while the counterfactual (resampled) replacement value is unlikely to be equally aligned.
- **derivation_steps**: Source states the equation is derived in the paper's Appendix B (not reproduced in the main-text excerpt read here — flagged as a gap, see below); the main text only supplies the resulting closed form and its qualitative interpretation (two labeled terms: rescaling of the PRE-EXISTING logit by the change in normalization scale, plus rescaling of the ablated head's OWN direct-effect change by the post-ablation scale) plus the intuitive argument for why S > S' typically holds.
- **worked_numbers**: Empirically, on head L11H2 in Pythia-160M (1M tokens, The Pile, filtered to top-2% of tokens by direct effect): the ratio S/S' (clean-to-ablated LayerNorm scale) is greater than 1 for 90.66% of tokens under resample ablation, 98.75% under mean ablation, and 100% under zero ablation — confirming S>S' (and hence LayerNorm-driven self-repair) is the dominant regime across all three standard ablation schemes.
- **source**: rushing-self-repair-2024.pdf
- **locator**: §3.1 'An Argument for LayerNorm Self-Repair' and §3.2 'Empirical Findings of LayerNorm Self-Repair', pp.4-5 (equation derived in Appendix B, not read in this pass)

### 18. Attribution patching as a first-order Taylor expansion of activation patching (confirms/extends existing C.2)
- **statement**: Activation patching's exact effect (running the model with one edge's activation replaced by a corrupted-run value) is approximated, to avoid a second forward pass per edge, by a first-order Taylor expansion of the loss/metric around the clean activation — the gradient-times-difference term is called the 'attribution score,' and its absolute value approximates the true patching effect. This linear approximation is measurably imperfect: on the docstring task it correlates only weakly with true activation patching (R²=0.27) and systematically OVERESTIMATES effect magnitude by roughly 2x (regression slope 0.531, i.e. true effect ≈ 0.531 × attribution-patching estimate).
- **equation_verbatim**: |L(x_clean | do(E=e_corr)) - L(x_clean)|   (1)

L(x_clean | do(E=e_corr)) ≈ L(x_clean) + (e_corr - e_clean)^T * [∂/∂e_clean] L(x_clean | do(E=e_clean))   (2)
[the second term is called Delta_e L, 'the attribution score']

Equation (1) is approximately equal to |Delta_e L|   (3)  ['the absolute attribution score']
- **symbols**: E = an edge in the model's computational graph (e.g. a specific attention-head-to-MLP dependency); e_clean, e_corr = the activation on that edge under the clean vs. corrupted prompt; L = the task metric (loss or logit difference); do(E=e_corr) = the do-operator patching edge E to its corrupted value; Delta_e L = the linear (first-order Taylor) attribution score for edge E.
- **assumptions**: The Taylor expansion truncates at first order (ignores curvature/higher-order terms), which is exact only in the limit e_corr -> e_clean; requires the gradient of L to be nonzero at e_clean (the paper notes this FAILS for a KL-divergence metric evaluated at the un-ablated model, since KL=0 there is a global minimum with zero gradient everywhere — motivating the use of task-specific, non-KL metrics instead).
- **derivation_steps**: Source derives Eq. (3) by 'a simple rearrangement' of Eq. (2) — i.e. subtracting L(x_clean) from both sides of the Taylor approximation and taking absolute value converts the approximation of L(...|do(E=e_corr)) into a direct approximation of the change in Eq. (1); the two forward passes (clean run to get e_clean and the loss, corrupted run to get e_corr) plus one backward pass (to get the gradient) replace the many additional forward passes activation patching would otherwise require per edge.
- **worked_numbers**: Docstring task case study: attribution-patching scores vs. true activation-patching scores across edges have correlation R²=0.27, with best-fit line y=0.531x−0.049 — i.e. attribution patching estimates the true patched effect as roughly TWICE as large as it actually is (slope 0.531 means the true/activation-patching value is about 0.531× the attribution-patching estimate). A single outlier edge (input to L1H4K) is traced via a lambda-interpolation study (0<=lambda<=1 mixing e_clean and e_corr) showing the TRUE interpolation curve is CONCAVE, so the linear (lambda=0 tangent) approximation systematically overestimates the effect of fully corrupting that edge.
- **source**: syed-eap-2023.pdf
- **locator**: §3.1-3.3, Eqs. (1)-(3), p.2-3; §5.1 'How faithful are Attribution Patching's approximations?', R²=0.27/slope=0.531 result and Fig. 4, p.7

### 19. Backup heads / OR-gate framing of self-repair, and why it makes patching-based importance measures systematically understate true importance
- **statement**: Backup components (e.g. a 'backup name mover head' in the IOI circuit) instantiate an OR-gate relationship with the primary component they back up: either one alone suffices for the behavior. Consequently, ABLATING the primary component makes it LOOK less important than it is (because the backup activates and compensates), even though the primary component genuinely matters when NEITHER is ablated — this is offered as the general mechanistic explanation for why ablation-based (total-effect) importance measures understate a component's true role whenever backup/self-repair behavior is present.
- **equation_verbatim**: Backup behaviour & OR-gates: [...] It can be helpful to think of these as OR-gates where either component is sufficient for the model to work. [...] Despite the name mover heads being important, if we ablate them then, due to backup heads compensating, the name movers look less important. Fortunately, backup behaviour seems to be lossy, i.e. if the original component boosted the logits by +X, the backup compensates for this by boosting less than X (the Hydra effect paper found 0.7*X). Thus these backup component[s] weaken the visibility of the original component, but it is usually still visible since even 0.3*X is a relatively large effect.
- **symbols**: X = the logit boost originally contributed by the primary (now-ablated) component; the backup component's compensating boost is characterized as < X (lossy compensation), leaving a residual visible effect of roughly (1-0.7)X = 0.3X in the cited example.
- **assumptions**: Frames backup/self-repair specifically as an OR-gate over the two (or more) redundant components — a simplification of the actual continuous compensation dynamics documented in McGrath et al. 2023's slope<1, layer-dependent compensatory-effect results (captured in a separate item above); the 0.7X figure is this guide's own paraphrase/citation of the McGrath et al. aggregate ~70% restoration finding, not an independently re-derived number.
- **derivation_steps**: source states without independent derivation, citing McGrath et al. 2023 (Hydra effect) and Wang et al. 2022 (IOI backup name mover heads) for the underlying empirical claim
- **worked_numbers**: '0.7*X' compensation, '0.3*X' residual visible effect — both are this guide's restatement of the McGrath et al. 2023 ~70%-restoration result (see the Compensatory-Effect item above for the primary source's own numbers: 92% variance explained at layer 23, slope<1 past layer 13, ~70% aggregate restoration at middle layers).
- **source**: heimersheim-activation-patching-guide-2024.pdf
- **locator**: §3.2 'Concepts & gotchas', 'Backup behaviour & OR-gates' paragraph, p.7
