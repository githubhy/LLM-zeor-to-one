### derivable_gaps
- THE HEADLINE GAP, and the one that earns the phrase 'first principles' for the whole appendix — NO source derives the exact minimizer of the SAE objective, so nothing connects the L1 penalty to the ReLU encoder, to shrinkage, to the threshold, or to JumpReLU. All four fall out of one calculation. Take unit-norm, MUTUALLY ORTHONORMAL decoder columns D (D^T D = I) and solve the *inference* problem the SAE amortizes: min_{f ≥ 0} ||x − Df||₂² + λ||f||₁. Orthonormality separates it coordinate-wise into min_{f_i ≥ 0} (f_i − (D^T x)_i)² + λ f_i, whose solution is the non-negative soft-threshold f_i* = ReLU((D^T x)_i − λ/2). Read off, in order: (a) the ReLU encoder of Cunningham Eq. (1) / Gated Eq. (2) is not an arbitrary design choice — it is the EXACT optimum in the orthonormal case, with W_enc = D^T and b_enc = −λ/2·1, i.e. the learned encoder is an amortized one-step inference network for this program; (b) shrinkage is exactly the additive −λ/2 shift, so Gated Fig. 2's λ=1 → 1/2 is the special case x=1; (c) detection and magnitude are entangled because ONE parameter λ sets both the threshold (λ/2) and the shift (λ/2) — which is the Gated paper's 'two roles' argument, stated only in prose in §3.1, here derived; (d) JumpReLU is precisely HARD-thresholding (threshold θ, no shift) against L1's SOFT-thresholding (threshold λ/2, shift λ/2), and decoupling θ from the shift is exactly what breaks the entanglement in (c). This single derivation is the spine; every architecture in the appendix is then a named point on it. Anchor it to Tibshirani 1996 (Gao cites it for shrinkage) and note the orthonormality assumption is FALSE for a real overcomplete SAE (M ≫ n forbids orthonormal columns) — so this is the tractable limit, and the appendix must say so rather than let it pass as general.
- The Gated-paper toy result f* = 1/2 is stated only as a figure caption; both the calculus and its GENERALIZATION are missing. The extractor supplied the one-line calculus correctly (minimize (1−f)² + λf, derivative −2(1−f)+λ = 0, f* = 1 − λ/2 = 1/2 at λ=1) — supply it, but also close the gap the extractor did not see: the toy bias is ADDITIVE (−λ/2, independent of x), whereas γ (Eq. (9)–(10)) measures a MULTIPLICATIVE rescaling. Those are different functional forms of the same phenomenon and the papers never reconcile them. Without this, a reader cannot tell whether γ=0.9 means '10% of every activation' or 'λ/2 of every activation'.
- The unit-norm decoder constraint is asserted by BOTH papers (Cunningham fn.3; Gated §2.2 'Following Bricken et al. (2023), we constrain columns to have exactly unit norm'; JumpReLU §2) and derived by NEITHER. It is a two-line proof and the appendix should give it: for any s > 0 the reparameterization (W_enc → W_enc/s, b_enc → b_enc/s, W_dec → s·W_dec) leaves x̂ pointwise unchanged and leaves the sparsity PATTERN unchanged, but scales ||f||₁ by 1/s; hence inf over the scale orbit of the L1 term is 0 and the unconstrained objective's infimum is the pure reconstruction loss — the penalty is vacuous, not merely weak. (6), from Conerly et al. 2024) removes the need for the constraint — the penalty is reparameterization-invariant by construction, which the JumpReLU paper asserts in one sentence and does not verify. Verify it: the orbit above scales f_i by 1/s and ||d_i||₂ by s.
- The γ closed form (Gated Eq. (10)) is explicitly a stated result — 'Explicitly solving the optimization problem in Eq. ### 1. Base SAE: linear dictionary ansatz and encoder/decoder
- **statement**: An activation x is modeled as (approximately) a sparse linear combination of unit-norm dictionary directions plus a bias/center term. A single-hidden-layer autoencoder with tied or untied weights parameterizes the encoder (producing sparse coefficients f, called c in Cunningham) and a linear decoder that reconstructs x from f.
- **equation_verbatim**: x ≈ x0 + Σ_{i=1}^{M} f_i(x) d_i   [Gated SAE Eq.1]
f(x) := ReLU(W_enc(x − b_dec) + b_enc)   [Gated SAE Eq.2]
x̂(f) := W_dec f + b_dec   [Gated SAE Eq.3]
--- Cunningham's original (tied-weight) form ---
c = ReLU(Mx + b)   (1)
x̂ = M^T c   (2)
x̂ = Σ_{i=0}^{d_hid − 1} c_i f_i   (3)
- **symbols**: x ∈ R^n (or R^din): model activation vector. x0: centering/bias term (≡ b_dec). M: number of dictionary/latent features. f_i(x) ≥ 0 (or c_i): sparse feature activation (coefficient) for feature i. d_i: unit-norm dictionary/feature direction (a decoder column). W_enc ∈ R^{M×n}, b_enc ∈ R^M: encoder weight and bias. W_dec ∈ R^{n×M}, b_dec ∈ R^n: decoder weight and bias. In Cunningham: M ∈ R^{d_hid × d_in} is the (tied) encoder=decoder^T weight matrix, b ∈ R^{d_hid} the encoder bias, d_hid = R·d_in with R a width-ratio hyperparameter, f_i (called dictionary features) are rows of M.
- **assumptions**: Ground-truth network features {g_j} are assumed to compose activations as a SPARSE linear combination (x_i = Σ_j a_i,j g_j with a_i sparse); the dictionary {f_k} is trying to approximate {g_j} 1-to-1. ReLU restricts f_i(x) ≥ 0. Tied weights (Cunningham) additionally assume the encoder direction that DETECTS a feature and the decoder direction that DEFINES it should coincide.
- **derivation_steps**: Both papers state the architecture directly as a design choice (analogy to classical sparse dictionary learning / Olshausen & Field); no derivation of WHY a single linear layer + ReLU is the right functional form is given beyond the linear-representation-hypothesis motivation in the prose.
- **worked_numbers**: Cunningham: din = 512 (Pythia-70M residual stream) or din = 1024 (Pythia-410M); R is the width-ratio hyperparameter (values swept in Fig. 7, e.g. dictionaries '4x overcomplete' before dead features appear, App. E).
- **source**: Cunningham et al., 'Sparse Autoencoders Find Highly Interpretable Features in Language Models' (2023); Rajamanoharan et al., 'Improving Dictionary Learning with Gated Sparse Autoencoders' (2024)
- **locator**: Cunningham §2, Eqs.(1)-(3), p.2; Gated SAE paper §2.1, Eqs.(1)-(3), p.2

### 2. Base SAE training loss (reconstruction + L1 sparsity)
- **statement**: SAEs are trained to jointly minimize reconstruction MSE and an L1 penalty on the feature activations, with a hyperparameter λ (α in Cunningham) trading the two off. Decoder columns must be norm-constrained or the L1 term can be trivially shrunk by rescaling encoder down / decoder up without changing the reconstruction or the sparsity pattern.
- **equation_verbatim**: Cunningham: L(x) = ||x − x̂||_2^2 + α||c||_1     (4)
Gated SAE / general form: L(x) := ||x − x̂(f(x))||_2^2 + λ||f(x)||_1     (4)
- **symbols**: α, λ: sparsity (L1) coefficient hyperparameter. ||x − x̂||_2^2: reconstruction loss. ||c||_1 = ||f(x)||_1: L1 sparsity penalty (sum of absolute feature activations; since f,c ≥ 0 under ReLU this equals the sum of the activations themselves).
- **assumptions**: Decoder (dictionary) columns are constrained to unit norm (Cunningham: 'Normalisation of the rows... prevents the model from reducing the sparsity loss term ||c||_1 by increasing the size of the feature vectors in M'). Without this constraint the loss is not well-posed under the joint (encoder, decoder) reparameterization x̂ = (sW)(d/s) for any scale s.
- **derivation_steps**: Cunningham states the loss and the row-normalization footnote as design choices, citing (without derivation in this paper) that L1-penalized reconstruction can recover ground-truth features 'empirically (Sharkey et al., 2023) and theoretically (Wright & Ma, 2022)'. No derivation of the recovery claim is given here — see the Identifiability item below.
- **worked_numbers**: none
- **source**: Cunningham et al. (2023)
- **locator**: §2, Eq.(4) and footnote 3, p.2-3

### 3. L1 shrinkage: mechanism and single-feature worked example
- **statement**: Holding the decoder fixed, the L1 penalty pushes feature activations f(x) toward zero while the reconstruction term pushes f(x) up to make x̂ accurate; the trained optimum lands strictly between the two, so activation MAGNITUDES are systematically underestimated ('shrinkage') even when the sparsity PATTERN (which features are active) is already correct — i.e. shrinkage buys no compensating sparsity benefit.
- **equation_verbatim**: (figure caption, worked numeric instance) 'E.g. a single-feature SAE (with L1 coefficient λ = 1) reconstructs 1/2 rather than 1 when minimizing Equation (4).'
- **symbols**: As in the base-SAE loss item above: λ the L1 coefficient; the '1' and '1/2' are the true feature activation value and the value the trained SAE actually outputs, respectively, for the toy single-feature case illustrated in Fig. 2.
- **assumptions**: The bias underlying this example: encoder output f(x) has two conflated roles — (1) DETECTING which features are active (binary: zero vs strictly positive — needs the L1 penalty to enforce sparsity) and (2) ESTIMATING the magnitude of active features (for which the L1 penalty is pure unwanted bias, with no sparsity benefit since the feature is already known active).
- **derivation_steps**: The paper states the qualitative mechanism in prose (§3.1) and gives the single-feature λ=1 → reconstructs-1/2-not-1 result as a stated fact/figure caption WITHOUT showing the intermediate calculus (i.e. it does not show the reader solving d/df[(x−f)^2 + λf] = 0 ⟹ f* = x − λ/2 for the case x=1, λ=1 giving f*=1/2). This is 'source states the result without derivation' — a straightforward one-line calculus derivation the survey should supply itself (minimize (1−f)^2 + f over f ≥ 0 gives f*=1/2 by setting the derivative −2(1−f)+1=0).
- **worked_numbers**: λ = 1, true activation = 1, SAE reconstructs f* = 1/2 (i.e. 50% shrinkage in this toy case).
- **source**: Rajamanoharan et al., 'Improving Dictionary Learning with Gated Sparse Autoencoders' (2024)
- **locator**: §3.1 'Motivation', p.3-4; Figure 2 caption, p.3

### 4. Relative reconstruction bias γ — quantifying shrinkage
- **statement**: The paper defines a scalar metric γ, the optimal multiplicative rescaling of an SAE's reconstructions that minimizes L2 reconstruction loss; γ=1 for an unbiased SAE and γ<1 indicates shrinkage. γ has a closed form in terms of only three scalar expectations (mean reconstruction norm, mean input norm, mean reconstruction loss), making it cheap to track during training.
- **equation_verbatim**: γ := arg min_{γ'} E_{x∼D}[ ||x̂_SAE(x)/γ' − x||_2^2 ]     (9)

γ = E_{x∼D}[||x̂_SAE(x)||_2^2] / E_{x∼D}[x̂_SAE(x)·x]
  = 2·E_{x∼D}[||x̂_SAE(x)||_2^2] / ( E_{x∼D}[||x̂_SAE(x)||_2^2] + E_{x∼D}[||x||_2^2] − E_{x∼D}[||x̂_SAE(x) − x||_2^2] )     (10)
- **symbols**: γ: relative reconstruction bias (γ<1 ⟺ shrinkage, per footnote 8's sign convention). x̂_SAE(x): the SAE's reconstruction of x. D: the data distribution of activations. γ': the dummy rescaling variable being optimized over.
- **assumptions**: The identity 2a·b ≡ ||a||^2 + ||b||^2 − ||a−b||^2 (footnote 9) is used to go from the first form of Eq.(10) to the second, avoiding the need to separately track the dot-product term E[x̂·x]. An unbiased-but-imperfect SAE (γ=1 but nonzero reconstruction loss) NECESSARILY has mean squared reconstruction norm strictly less than the mean squared input norm — shrinkage makes this gap even larger.
- **derivation_steps**: The paper states 'Explicitly solving the optimization problem in Eq.(9)... can be expressed analytically' and gives the closed-form result directly (Eq.10) without showing the intermediate calculus. The derivation the survey must supply: differentiate E[||x̂/γ' − x||^2] w.r.t. γ', set to zero: d/dγ'[ E||x̂||^2/γ'^2 − 2E[x̂·x]/γ' + E||x||^2 ] = −2E||x̂||^2/γ'^3 + 2E[x̂·x]/γ'^2 = 0 ⟹ γ' = E||x̂||^2 / E[x̂·x], which is the first equality of Eq.(10); the second equality then substitutes the polarization identity 2·E[x̂·x] = E||x̂||^2 + E||x||^2 − E||x̂−x||^2 into the denominator.
- **worked_numbers**: Empirical table (Gemma-7B Gated SAEs, App., 'Shrinkage γ' column): values mostly in [0.98, 1.15] across sparsity/L0 settings, with one row at γ=2.56 (an outlier at very low % alive features, indicating severe shrinkage/instability when many latents are dead).
- **source**: Rajamanoharan et al. (2024), Gated SAE paper
- **locator**: §4.2, Eqs.(9)-(10) and footnotes 8-9, p.6-7; Table 3/App. for γ values

### 5. Gated SAE architecture: decoupling detection from magnitude
- **statement**: The Gated SAE replaces the single ReLU encoder with a GATED ReLU encoder: a Heaviside gate f_gate (binary: is the feature active?) elementwise-multiplies a ReLU magnitude estimate f_mag (how large is it?). Only f_gate is penalized for sparsity, so the L1 bias only touches the DETECTION pathway. To avoid doubling parameters, the magnitude-path weights are defined as a per-feature exponential rescaling of the (shared) gate-path weights.
- **equation_verbatim**: f̃(x) := 1[(W_gate(x − b_dec) + b_gate) > 0] ⊙ ReLU(W_mag(x − b_dec) + b_mag)     (6)
(f_gate(x) is the first factor, f_mag(x) the second; π_gate(x) := W_gate(x − b_dec) + b_gate are the fgate sub-layer's pre-activations)

(W_mag)_{ij} := exp(r_mag)_i · (W_gate)_{ij}     (7)
- **symbols**: 1[•>0]: pointwise Heaviside step function. ⊙: elementwise multiplication. W_gate, b_gate: gate-path encoder weight/bias. W_mag, b_mag: magnitude-path encoder weight/bias. r_mag ∈ R^M: a learned per-feature log-scale rescaling parameter tying W_mag to W_gate. π_gate(x): gate sub-layer pre-activations (used in the loss, see next item).
- **assumptions**: Weight sharing (Eq.7) assumes the optimal gate and magnitude PROJECTION DIRECTIONS coincide and only their norms/biases should differ — this is an explicit modeling choice made to keep the parameter overhead to only 2×M extra parameters versus a baseline SAE (rather than fully doubling encoder parameters).
- **derivation_steps**: Architecture is stated directly (design choice), motivated by Gated Linear Units (Dauphin et al. 2017; Shazeer 2020). Appendix E (referenced, not fully quoted here) shows that after applying the weight-tying scheme, the gated encoder is algebraically equivalent to a single-layer linear encoder followed by a JumpReLU with threshold θ = b_mag − e^{r_mag} ⊙ b_gate — i.e. Gated SAEs and JumpReLU SAEs are the SAME function class at inference time, differing only in how they are trained.
- **worked_numbers**: 'the Gated SAE has only 2×M more parameters than a baseline SAE' (vs. a naive doubling to 2× the baseline).
- **source**: Rajamanoharan et al. (2024), Gated SAE paper
- **locator**: §3.2.1, Eqs.(6)-(7), p.4-5; Fig.3, Fig.4

### 6. Gated SAE loss: why the naive loss fails, and the auxiliary-task fix
- **statement**: Naively restricting the L1 penalty to f_gate breaks because the Heaviside step inside f_gate has zero gradient almost everywhere, so no gradient reaches W_gate, b_gate at all. The fix: (i) apply the L1 penalty to the RECTIFIED pre-activations ReLU(π_gate(x)) instead of to the (non-differentiable) gated output, and (ii) add an auxiliary reconstruction task that trains the gate path to itself be reconstruction-useful, using a FROZEN copy of the decoder so its gradients cannot leak into and corrupt the main decoder.
- **equation_verbatim**: L_incorrect(x) := ||x − x̂(f̃(x))||_2^2 + λ||f_gate(x)||_1     [naive/broken version — no eq. number given]

L_gated(x) := ||x − x̂(f̃(x))||_2^2 + λ||ReLU(π_gate(x))||_1 + ||x − x̂_frozen(ReLU(π_gate(x)))||_2^2     (8)
           |________Lreconstruct________|   |______Lsparsity______|   |___________Laux___________|

where x̂_frozen(f) := W_dec^copy f + b_dec^copy
- **symbols**: L_reconstruct: main reconstruction term (same as baseline). L_sparsity: L1 penalty now on rectified gate pre-activations, so it DOES have a nonzero subgradient w.r.t. W_gate, b_gate wherever π_gate(x)>0. L_aux: auxiliary reconstruction loss using ReLU(π_gate(x)) directly as the latent code and a stop-gradiented ('frozen') copy of the decoder. x̂_frozen: decoder applied with copied (non-trainable-through-this-path) weights/bias.
- **assumptions**: Stop-gradient (or explicit frozen copy) on W_dec, b_dec inside L_aux is essential — without it, gradients from L_aux would flow into and distort the MAIN decoder used for L_reconstruct.
- **derivation_steps**: The paper walks through the failure mode explicitly: states L_incorrect, explains 'due to the Heaviside step activation function in f_gate, no gradients would propagate to W_gate and b_gate', then motivates each of the two fixes in turn (rectified-preactivation L1 for a nonzero-almost-everywhere sparsity gradient; auxiliary frozen-decoder task so the gate path also learns to be useful for reconstruction, not just sparse). No further calculus derivation of gradients is shown.
- **worked_numbers**: Computing L_gated requires running the decoder TWICE per step (once for Lreconstruct, once for Laux), giving 'a 50% increase in the compute required to perform a training update step' versus baseline SAE training.
- **source**: Rajamanoharan et al. (2024), Gated SAE paper
- **locator**: §3.2.2, Eq.(8), p.5-6

### 7. JumpReLU activation and JumpReLU SAE
- **statement**: The JumpReLU activation zeroes pre-activations below a learned per-feature threshold θ and passes them through as the identity above threshold (no rescaling of surviving activations, unlike a shifted ReLU) — this directly gives an SAE encoder that decouples the fire/don't-fire decision (governed by θ) from the magnitude of an active feature (governed directly by the raw pre-activation, undistorted).
- **equation_verbatim**: π(x) := W_enc x + b_enc     (3)
JumpReLU_θ(z) := z·H(z − θ)     (4)
f(x) := JumpReLU_θ(W_enc x + b_enc)     (8)
- **symbols**: π(x): encoder pre-activations. z: a scalar pre-activation. θ ∈ R_+^M: per-feature threshold vector, one entry per latent i. H: Heaviside step function (H(z)=1 for z>0, 0 for z<0; value at z=0 is 'a matter of convention — unimportant when H appears within integrals or integral estimators').
- **assumptions**: θ > 0 componentwise (a positive vector-valued parameter). Note JumpReLU_θ(z) is discontinuous at z=θ (it jumps from 0 to θ as z crosses θ from below), which is exactly why its exact derivative w.r.t. θ is a (non-trainable) Dirac delta — motivating the pseudo-derivative/STE machinery below.
- **derivation_steps**: Stated directly as a definition; the paper notes JumpReLU SAEs generalize both baseline ReLU SAEs (θ=0 recovers ReLU) and are shown (citing Rajamanoharan et al. 2024 App. E) to be exactly what a weight-tied Gated SAE reduces to at inference time.
- **worked_numbers**: none (definitional)
- **source**: Rajamanoharan et al., 'Jumping Ahead: Improving Reconstruction Fidelity with JumpReLU Sparse Autoencoders' (2024)
- **locator**: §2, Eq.(3); §3, Eqs.(4),(8), p.3-4

### 8. JumpReLU SAE loss: L0 sparsity penalty and the reparameterization-invariant L1 alternative
- **statement**: JumpReLU SAEs are trained with an explicit L0 penalty (the true count of active features) rather than the L1 surrogate, since the threshold θ itself now directly enforces/controls sparsity, making the L1 approximation's shrinkage bias unnecessary. The paper also introduces a reparameterization-INVARIANT L1 penalty (RI-L1) — weighting each activation by its OWN decoder-direction norm — as an alternative fix usable when a genuine L1 penalty is still wanted (e.g. for Gated SAEs), since a standard (unweighted) L1 penalty can be arbitrarily shrunk by rescaling encoder down / decoder up without any real change to sparsity or reconstruction.
- **equation_verbatim**: General SAE loss form: L(x) := ||x − x̂(f(x))||_2^2 + λS(f(x)) + Laux     (5)

Reparameterization-invariant L1 (RI-L1): S_RI-L1(f) := Σ_{i=1}^{M} f_i ||d_i||_2     (6)

JumpReLU SAE training loss: L(x) := ||x − x̂(f(x))||_2^2 + λ||f(x)||_0     (9)
- **symbols**: S(f): a general sparsity-penalizing function of the feature-coefficient vector. Laux: optional auxiliary loss terms (used differently by different architectures — the Gated auxiliary reconstruction task, or TopK's AuxK dead-latent loss). d_i: the i-th dictionary direction (decoder column), which may not be unit-norm under RI-L1 since RI-L1 removes the NEED to constrain ||d_i||=1. ||f(x)||_0: the true count of nonzero (active) feature coefficients.
- **assumptions**: For Eq.(9) to be trainable at all, θ needs a gradient signal, but θ appears ONLY inside Heaviside-step arguments in both L_reconstruct (via JumpReLU_θ) and L_sparsity (via ||f||_0's implicit Heaviside), so the naive gradient is a Dirac delta — motivating the pseudo-derivative item below. TopK SAEs (cited as a comparison) are trained WITHOUT any sparsity penalty term at all, since the TopK activation function directly and exactly enforces the desired L0.
- **derivation_steps**: The paper states these forms directly; it separately proves (footnote/prose) that a plain (unweighted) L1 penalty is NOT invariant under the reparameterization W_enc → sW_enc, W_dec → W_dec/s (which leaves f and x̂ unchanged but scales ||f||_1 by 1/s), hence the motivation for RI-L1, which is by construction scale-invariant since f_i||d_i|| is unchanged under this same rescaling (f_i→f_i/s exactly cancels ||d_i||→s||d_i||... i.e. the product is preserved). This cancellation is stated as the defining property but the explicit algebra is not spelled out in the source text extracted.
- **worked_numbers**: none
- **source**: Rajamanoharan et al., JumpReLU SAE paper (2024)
- **locator**: §2 'Sparsity penalties', Eqs.(5)-(6), p.3; §3, Eq.(9), p.4

### 9. Straight-through pseudo-derivatives for the JumpReLU threshold
- **statement**: Because both JumpReLU_θ(z) and H(z−θ) are piecewise-constant in θ (zero true gradient almost everywhere), the paper defines KERNEL-based pseudo-derivatives — a rescaled kernel function evaluated at (z−θ)/ε — to stand in for the (Dirac-delta) true derivatives during backpropagation, giving θ a usable gradient signal whenever a pre-activation lands within a small window of width ε around the threshold.
- **equation_verbatim**: ∂/∂θ JumpReLU_θ(z) := −(θ/ε)·K((z−θ)/ε)     (11)
∂/∂θ H(z − θ) := −(1/ε)·K((z−θ)/ε)     (12)
(the paper writes ð/ð to distinguish 'pseudo-derivative' from a true partial derivative ∂/∂z)
- **symbols**: K: any valid kernel function (must satisfy the properties of a centered, finite-variance probability density function, e.g. ∫x^2K(x)dx > 0). ε: the KDE bandwidth hyperparameter — 'too low and gradient estimates become too noisy, too high and estimates become too biased'. In the paper's experiments K = rect(z) := H(z+1/2) − H(z−1/2) (the rectangle/boxcar kernel).
- **assumptions**: K must be a valid (centered, finite-variance) kernel in the classical KDE sense. The pseudo-derivative is applied ONLY on the backward pass (the forward pass still computes the exact, non-differentiable JumpReLU/Heaviside) — this is the 'straight-through estimator' (STE) idea from Bengio et al. (2013).
- **derivation_steps**: The paper gives the definitions directly as 'we define the following pseudo-derivative', without a from-scratch derivation at this point in the main text — BUT the follow-on §4 (next item) supplies the actual justification: it shows these pseudo-derivatives are exactly the batch-wise mean gradient of a kernel-density estimator of the TRUE gradient of the EXPECTED loss (which, unlike the pointwise loss, is smooth in θ). The paper's own worked/implementation-verified reference (Appendix J pseudocode, `jumprelu_bwd`) confirms these formulas are used verbatim: `threshold_grad = -(threshold/bandwidth) * rectangle((x-threshold)/bandwidth) * output_grad` for JumpReLU and the analogous line (without the leading θ factor) for the Heaviside/step function.
- **worked_numbers**: ε = 0.001 found to 'work well across different models, layers and sites' (assuming a dataset normalized such that E_x[x^2] = 1), found by sweeping.
- **source**: Rajamanoharan et al., JumpReLU SAE paper (2024)
- **locator**: §3 (unlabeled subsection before §4), Eqs.(11)-(12) and Fig.4, p.4-5; Appendix J pseudocode, p.24-25

### 10. Why the STE works: analytic gradient of the expected loss and its KDE estimator
- **statement**: Although the PER-SAMPLE JumpReLU loss is piecewise-constant in θ (zero gradient), the EXPECTED loss E_x[L_θ(x)] over the data distribution is smooth in θ, because θ moving continuously sweeps a continuously-varying PROBABILITY MASS of samples across the jump. The paper derives the true analytic gradient of this expected loss in closed form, then shows that estimating it via a Nadaraya-Watson-style kernel-density estimator over a minibatch produces EXACTLY the STE pseudo-derivative rule of Eqs.(11)-(12).
- **equation_verbatim**: ∂E_x[L_θ(x)] / ∂θ_i = ( E_x[I_i(x) | π_i(x) = θ_i] − λ ) p_i(θ_i)     (13)
where I_i(x) := 2θ_i · d_i · (x − x̂(f(x)))     (14)

Batch estimator of Eq.(13):
(1/(Nε)) Σ_{α=1}^{N} { I_i(x_α) − λ } K( (π_i(x_α) − θ_i) / ε )     (15)
- **symbols**: p_i: probability density function of the distribution of feature i's pre-activations π_i(x). I_i(x): a per-sample quantity ('intuitively, the first term in Eq.(13) measures the rate at which the expected reconstruction loss would increase if we increase θ_i'), built from the decoder direction d_i and the current reconstruction residual x − x̂(f(x)). N: minibatch size, x_1,...,x_N the batch samples. λ: the sparsity coefficient (same λ as in Eq.9's ||f(x)||_0 term).
- **assumptions**: Eq.(13) is derived (per the paper, in its Appendix B, not reproduced in the extracted text) by differentiating the expected loss analytically; the key structural fact enabling this is that as θ_i is perturbed, only samples with pre-activation EXACTLY at θ_i change which side of the jump they fall on, so the derivative is a boundary/surface term weighted by the local density p_i(θ_i) — the standard mechanism behind differentiating through an indicator/threshold in expectation.
- **derivation_steps**: The paper states: 'As shown in Appendix B, we can differentiate expected loss with respect to θ analytically to obtain [Eq.13]' (i.e. it POINTS to an appendix derivation rather than reproducing the steps in the main text; that appendix was not captured in this extraction pass — flagged as a further-detail gap below). It then states that using a 'generalised KDE estimator of the form Eq.(7)' [a general kernel-density-estimator formula for a conditional-expectation-times-density quantity, given earlier in §2] on Eq.(13) yields the batch estimator Eq.(15), and finally states (without reproducing the check) that 'when we instruct autograd to use the pseudo-derivatives defined in Eqs.(11) and (12) in the backward pass, this is precisely the batch-wise mean gradient' of Eq.(15) — i.e. the STE recipe is proven equivalent to KDE-estimating the true expected-loss gradient.
- **worked_numbers**: none beyond ε=0.001 (already reported above)
- **source**: Rajamanoharan et al., JumpReLU SAE paper (2024)

### 11. TopK SAE: objective and activation function
- **statement**: The TopK SAE enforces sparsity directly and exactly by zeroing all but the k largest encoder pre-activations (a hard k-sparse constraint), removing the need for any L1 penalty or λ hyperparameter at all — the training loss reduces to pure reconstruction MSE, since the sparsity level is now set structurally by k rather than tuned via a penalty coefficient.
- **equation_verbatim**: Baseline ReLU SAE (for comparison): z = ReLU(W_enc(x − b_pre) + b_enc); x̂ = W_dec z + b_pre     (1)
L = ||x − x̂||_2^2 + λ||z||_1   [baseline loss]

TopK SAE encoder: z = TopK(W_enc(x − b_pre))     (2)
TopK SAE training loss: L = ||x − x̂||_2^2   [stated in prose immediately after Eq.2, no separate eq. number]
- **symbols**: TopK(·): activation function keeping only the k largest components of its input vector, zeroing the rest. k: the fixed sparsity level (number of active latents per sample) — a hyperparameter set directly, not tuned indirectly via λ. W_enc ∈ R^{n×d}, b_enc ∈ R^n, W_dec ∈ R^{d×n}, b_pre ∈ R^d (using the paper's own dimension convention: n latent dims, d = d_model). Note: the decoder in Eq.(2)'s TopK model is 'unchanged' from the baseline (same x̂ = W_dec z + b_pre form).
- **assumptions**: The paper notes (footnote 5) that in their actual code they ALSO apply a ReLU before TopK to guarantee non-negative activations, but state 'the training curves are indistinguishable, as the k largest activations are almost always positive for reasonable choices of k' — i.e. the ReLU is a near-no-op safety net, not load-bearing.
- **derivation_steps**: Stated directly as a design choice (citing Makhzani & Frey 2013's original k-sparse autoencoder). The paper enumerates the BENEFITS as a bulleted list rather than a derivation: removes the L1-shrinkage bias ('L1... introduces a bias of shrinking all positive activations toward zero'); sets L0 directly rather than indirectly via λ; empirically Pareto-dominates ReLU baselines and the gap widens with scale; increases monosemanticity by clamping small activations exactly to zero.
- **worked_numbers**: none in this item (see joint scaling-law item for the concrete numeric fit)
- **source**: Gao et al., 'Scaling and Evaluating Sparse Autoencoders' (2024) [same file as gao-topk-saes-2024.pdf, confirmed identical by md5]
- **locator**: §2.2, Eq.(1); §2.3, Eq.(2), p.2-3

### 12. Gao et al. joint scaling law for reconstruction MSE vs. dictionary size and sparsity
- **statement**: MSE follows a joint power-law-like relationship in both the number of latents n and the sparsity level k, fit on GPT-4-family autoencoders; this scaling law is only valid in the small-k regime (reconstruction becomes trivial as k approaches d_model). The fit shows the scaling law L(n) gets STEEPER (more negative exponent-like coefficient) as k increases, and the irreducible-loss term DECREASES as k increases.
- **equation_verbatim**: L(n, k) = exp(α + β_k·log(k) + β_n·log(n) + γ·log(k)·log(n)) + exp(ζ + η·log(k))     (3)
with α = −0.50, β_k = 0.26, β_n = −0.017, γ = −0.042, ζ = −1.32, η = −0.085.
- **symbols**: L(n,k): normalized MSE as a function of dictionary size n and sparsity level k. α, β_k, β_n, γ, ζ, η: fitted scalar coefficients (γ is the cross/interaction term between log(k) and log(n); the second exp(ζ+η·log(k)) term is the irreducible-loss floor, itself a function of k only).
- **assumptions**: Holds only for the small-k regime (as k → d_model, reconstruction trivializes and the power law breaks down). Fit is specifically on GPT-4-family subject models (not GPT-2 small, whose Θ(n^0.6) token-budget-to-convergence exponent the paper separately reports differs from GPT-4's Θ(n^0.65)).
- **derivation_steps**: Stated as an empirical fit ('Our joint scaling law fit on GPT-4 autoencoders is: [Eq.3]') — no first-principles derivation is given (or claimed); this is a purely empirical/statistical scaling-law fit, in the tradition of Kaplan et al. (2020)/Hoffmann et al. (2022) LM scaling laws, extended here with an added irreducible-loss term because a pure power law under-fit the smallest models. The paper separately notes (§3.1.3) that including an irreducible-loss term e in y = αx^β + e 'substantially improves the quality of our fits' — motivating the two-exp-term structure of Eq.(3) but not deriving its specific functional form from anything more fundamental.
- **worked_numbers**: α=−0.50, β_k=0.26, β_n=−0.017, γ=−0.042, ζ=−1.32, η=−0.085 (all six coefficients of the GPT-4-family fit). Also: largest learning rate that converges scales with 1/√n; tokens-to-convergence ~ Θ(n^0.6) for GPT-2 small vs Θ(n^0.65) for GPT-4.
- **source**: Gao et al., 'Scaling and Evaluating Sparse Autoencoders' (2024)
- **locator**: §3.1.4, Eq.(3), p.4

### 13. Dead latent problem: original observation and its scale
- **statement**: As dictionary width grows, an increasing fraction of latents stop activating entirely partway through training ('dead latents') and never recover; this both wastes capacity (fewer than the full width is actually usable as an overcomplete basis) and worsens reconstruction MSE. The problem was already observed in the very first SAE-for-LLM-interpretability paper (Cunningham et al.) in MLP-layer dictionaries, and later papers report it becomes severe at large dictionary scale without explicit mitigation.
- **equation_verbatim**: (descriptive, no closed-form equation in the source) — Cunningham's untied-encoder mitigation for MLP layers:
c = ReLU(M_e x + b)     (5)
x̂ = M_d^T c     (6)
- **symbols**: M_e, M_d: SEPARATE encoder and decoder weight matrices (untying them, versus the tied M=M_d^T=M_e of Eqs.1-3, is Cunningham's partial mitigation for MLP-layer dead features).
- **assumptions**: Cunningham's diagnosis: 'Due to the large part of the input space that is never used due to the non-linearity, it is much easier for MLP dictionary features to become stuck at a position where they hardly ever activate' — i.e. the ReLU's flat zero region for negative pre-activations creates a basin a feature's weights can fall into and never receive gradient signal to escape.
- **derivation_steps**: Cunningham states the observation and the untied-weights partial fix in prose, with no formal mechanism proof; explicitly defers a full fix to future work ('we plan to reinitialise such 'dead features' to ensure we learn as many useful dictionary features as possible'). Gao et al. give the later, quantified picture: up to 90% dead latents 'when no mitigations are applied' at large scale, versus only 7% dead in their largest (16-million-latent) autoencoder once BOTH of their two mitigations (transpose-init + AuxK, see next item) are applied together.
- **worked_numbers**: Cunningham: 'in a dictionary with twice as many features as neurons, less than half might be active enough to perform automatic interpretability' (MLP layers); residual-stream dictionaries stay '4x overcomplete before some features start to drop out completely' (App. E) with hyperparameters α=8.6e−4 (residual) / α=3.2e−4 (MLP). Gao et al.: up to 90% dead with no mitigation; only 7% dead in the 16M-latent model with both mitigations; a dead latent is defined (following Templeton et al. 2024) as one that 'has not activated in 10 million tokens'.
- **source**: Cunningham et al. (2023); Gao et al. (2024)
- **locator**: Cunningham §C.3 and App.E, p.9,17; Gao et al. §2.4, p.2-3

### 14. AuxK auxiliary loss for preventing dead latents
- **statement**: AuxK models the MAIN model's reconstruction ERROR using only the top-k_aux latents AMONG THE DEAD ones — this gives dead latents a training signal (fit the residual the live latents are missing) without letting them corrupt the primary reconstruction, and the resulting auxiliary loss is added to the main loss with a small scaling coefficient. Combined with transpose-initializing the encoder to the decoder's transpose, this is the two-ingredient recipe that suppresses dead latents even at very large scale.
- **equation_verbatim**: e = x − x̂     [main-model reconstruction error]
ê = W_dec z     [reconstruction using the top-k_aux DEAD latents]
L_aux = ||e − ê||_2^2
Full loss: L + α·L_aux
- **symbols**: e: the main model's residual reconstruction error. z (in this equation): the activation vector restricted to / computed from only the top-k_aux dead latents (i.e. TopK applied with k=k_aux, but only over the indices currently flagged dead). ê: the auxiliary reconstruction built purely from those dead-latent activations. α: a small scaling coefficient for the auxiliary loss (typically 1/32). k_aux: number of dead latents used in the auxiliary reconstruction (typically 512).
- **assumptions**: A latent is FLAGGED dead 'if it has not activated for some predetermined number of tokens (typically 10 million)'. The AuxK loss 'very occasionally NaNs at large scale', and is zeroed out when NaN to prevent training-run collapse (an explicit numerical-safety patch, not part of the core math).
- **derivation_steps**: Stated directly as a design (an analogy to 'ghost grads', Jermyn & Templeton 2024) with the mechanism explained in one sentence but no formal proof that it prevents dead latents; justification is entirely empirical ('We found that using AuxK eliminates almost all dead latents by the end of training', citing their own ablation Figure 15).
- **worked_numbers**: k_aux = 512 (typical); α = 1/32 (typical); dead-latent flag threshold = 10 million tokens without activating; adding AuxK increases computational cost by 'about 10%' (since the encoder forward pass, which dominates cost, is shared between main and auxiliary reconstructions).
- **source**: Gao et al. (2024)
- **locator**: Appendix A.2, p.17 (also restated identically, with the same k_aux and α defaults, in Bussmann et al. 2024 §2, p.2 for context)

### 15. BatchTopK: batch-level sparsity allocation and inference-time threshold
- **statement**: BatchTopK relaxes TopK's rigid PER-SAMPLE k-sparsity constraint to a PER-BATCH constraint: instead of keeping exactly the top k activations for every individual sample, it keeps the top n·k activations across an entire batch of n samples, letting some samples use more than k latents and others fewer, adaptively. Because this couples samples together (a dependency the model can't use at single-sample inference time), a single GLOBAL threshold θ is estimated post hoc — the average, across batches, of the SMALLEST POSITIVE activation surviving the BatchTopK cut — and a JumpReLU(θ) activation is substituted at inference.
- **equation_verbatim**: L(X) = ||X − (BatchTopK(W_enc X + b_enc)W_dec + b_dec))||_2^2 + αLaux     (6)

θ = E_X[ min{ z_{i,j}(X) | z_{i,j}(X) > 0 } ]     (7)
- **symbols**: X: a full BATCH of input activations (not a single sample). BatchTopK(·): the batch-level top-(n·k) operator, n = batch size, k = target average sparsity per sample. z_{i,j}(X): the j-th latent's activation for the i-th sample in batch X. θ: the single global inference-time threshold, estimated as the batch-average of each batch's minimum surviving-positive activation. L_aux: 'the same as in TopK SAEs' (i.e. the AuxK loss above).
- **assumptions**: At inference (single-sample, no batch to pool sparsity across), BatchTopK itself is undefined/inapplicable — the paper's fix is to swap in JumpReLU_θ using the ESTIMATED θ from Eq.(7), decoupling training-time (batch-relative) sparsity allocation from inference-time (per-sample, threshold-based) sparsity.
- **derivation_steps**: Both equations are stated directly as the proposed method, without further derivation; Eq.(7) is presented as a plausible/empirically-validated ESTIMATOR (an average of per-batch minima) rather than derived from an optimality criterion.
- **worked_numbers**: none given for θ's numeric value in the extracted text; the paper reports BatchTopK 'consistently outperforms TopK and JumpReLU SAEs in terms of reconstruction fidelity' at matched average sparsity (Fig.1-2), and cites k_aux=512, α=1/32 as inherited AuxK defaults.
- **source**: Bussmann et al., 'BatchTopK Sparse Autoencoders' (2024)
- **locator**: §3, Eqs.(6)-(7), p.3

### 17. Feature absorption: hierarchical-feature toy setup (formal preliminaries)
- **statement**: The paper sets up a fully specified toy model — mutually orthogonal unit-norm true features, a hierarchy relation where a 'child' feature's presence logically implies the 'parent' feature's presence, and a bias-free single-hidden-layer SAE — as the substrate for a formal proof that gradient descent on the standard SAE loss actively PREFERS a pathological ('absorbed') encoder/decoder solution over the naive ground-truth-recovering one, whenever the two features co-occur with nonzero probability.
- **equation_verbatim**: H1 (Dataset/Features): f_i ∈ R^k, ||f_i||_2 = 1 (unit norm); f_i · f_j = δ_ij (mutually orthogonal). h = Σ_{j∈ActiveFeatures} f_j.

H2 (Hierarchy f2 ⊂ f1, i.e. f2 firing ⟹ f1 firing): p(f1,f2)=p11, p(f1,¬f2)=p10, p(¬f1,f2)=p01=0 (by hierarchy), p(¬f1,¬f2)=p00; p11+p10+p00=1.

H3 (SAE model, no biases): ĥ = W_d z, z = ReLU(W_e h); W_{e,i} = i-th row (encoder vector for latent i), W_{d,i} = i-th column (decoder vector for latent i) of W_e, W_d respectively. Latents j>2 assumed to perfectly recover f_j (W_{e,j}=f_j, W_{d,j}=f_j) and not interact with f1,f2 by orthogonality.

H4 (Loss): L = L_rec + λL_sp, λ>0; L_rec = E_h∼D[||h−ĥ||_2^2]; L_sp = E_h∼D[Σ_i |z_i|].

H5 (δ-absorption parameterization, δ∈[0,1]): W_{e,1} = f1 − δf2; W_{e,2} = f2; W_{d,1} = f1; W_{d,2} = f2 + δf1.
- **symbols**: F = {f1,...,fd}: the set of orthonormal true feature directions. h: an activation, a sum of the currently-active true feature directions. z, ĥ: the SAE's latent activations and reconstruction. δ ∈ [0,1]: the absorption parameter — δ=0 is 'no absorption' (ground-truth recovery), δ=1 is 'full absorption'. p11, p10, p01, p00: the joint firing-pattern probabilities of the parent/child pair (f1,f2).
- **assumptions**: Features are exactly orthogonal and unit-norm (idealized — no superposition noise within this pair). The SAE has NO bias terms in this analysis. Only latents 1 and 2 are examined; all other latents j>2 are ASSUMED to already perfectly track their own orthogonal feature f_j and not interact — i.e. the proof isolates the two-feature hierarchical subproblem from the rest of the dictionary.
- **derivation_steps**: This is the paper's own from-scratch formal setup (its own original contribution, not summarizing a citation) — stated as hypotheses H1-H5 to be used in the two propositions that follow (see next two items).
- **worked_numbers**: The paper's empirical (not purely-formal) toy experiment uses: 4 true features, 50-dim representation, unit firing magnitude; f0 fires with probability 0.25, f1/f2/f3 each fire with probability 0.05 (independent case); in the hierarchical case f1 is modified to fire ONLY when f0 also fires, keeping f1's marginal firing rate at 5%; L1 coefficient 3e-5, learning rate 3e-4, trained on 100M activations, 4-latent SAE via SAELens.
- **source**: Chanin et al., 'A is for Absorption: Studying Feature Splitting and Absorption in Sparse Autoencoders' (2024)
- **locator**: §3 (empirical toy setup), p.3; Appendix A.2 (formal H1-H5), p.15-16

### 18. Feature absorption Proposition 1: perfect reconstruction is preserved at every absorption level
- **statement**: For ANY δ∈[0,1] and for every input composed only of f1, f2, or the zero vector (the only inputs the hierarchy assumption permits), the δ-parameterized encoder/decoder reconstructs the input EXACTLY. This is the key fact that makes absorption 'free' from the reconstruction loss's point of view — δ is a degree of freedom the reconstruction term is totally indifferent to.
- **equation_verbatim**: Case h=f1: z1 = ReLU((f1−δf2)·f1) = ReLU(1−δ·0) = 1; z2 = ReLU(f2·f1) = ReLU(0) = 0. ĥ = 1·f1 + 0·(f2+δf1) = f1 = h.

Case h=f1+f2: z1 = ReLU((f1−δf2)·(f1+f2)) = ReLU(1+0−δ·0−δ·1) = ReLU(1−δ) = 1−δ (since δ∈[0,1]); z2 = ReLU(f2·(f1+f2)) = ReLU(0+1) = 1. ĥ = (1−δ)f1 + 1·(f2+δf1) = (1−δ)f1+f2+δf1 = f1+f2 = h.

Case h=0: z1=z2=0; ĥ=0=h.

(Case h=f2 alone is disallowed by H2, since p01=0.)
- **symbols**: As defined in the toy-setup item (H1-H5). z1, z2: latent activations of the two examined latents.
- **assumptions**: Same as H1-H5 above (orthonormal features, no bias, the specific δ-parameterization of W_e,W_d).
- **derivation_steps**: Full, complete step-by-step algebra is given in the source for all three permissible cases (h=f1; h=f1+f2; h=0), each computed by direct substitution of the H5 weight definitions into z=ReLU(W_e·h) then ĥ=z1·W_d,1+z2·W_d,2, using orthonormality (f_i·f_j=δ_ij) to simplify every dot product. This is a genuinely complete, first-principles derivation already — nothing further needs to be supplied.
- **worked_numbers**: n/a — this is itself a fully worked symbolic derivation, δ enters but cancels exactly in the h=f1+f2 case: (1−δ)f1+δf1 = f1.
- **source**: Chanin et al. (2024)
- **locator**: Appendix A.2, 'Proposition 1', p.15-16

### 19. Feature absorption Proposition 2 + Corollary: sparsity loss strictly favors full absorption
- **statement**: The expected sparsity-loss contribution from the two examined latents is an EXPLICIT LINEAR function of δ with slope exactly −p11 — so whenever the parent and child co-occur with nonzero probability (p11>0), increasing δ strictly decreases the sparsity loss with no reconstruction-loss penalty (Prop.1), meaning gradient descent on the combined loss L=L_rec+λL_sp is driven all the way to δ=1 (full absorption) rather than the ground-truth-recovering δ=0.
- **equation_verbatim**: |z1|+|z2| per case: h=f1+f2 (prob p11): (1−δ)+1 = 2−δ. h=f1 (prob p10): 1+0 = 1. h=0 (prob p00): 0.

L_sp^(1,2) = p11·(2−δ) + p10·1 + p00·0 = p11(2−δ) + p10

dL_sp^(1,2)/dδ = −p11

Corollary: if p11>0, L_sp^(1,2) is a strictly decreasing function of δ on [0,1]; minimum at δ=1: L_sp^(1,2)(δ=1) = p11+p10; maximum at δ=0: L_sp^(1,2)(δ=0) = 2p11+p10.
- **symbols**: L_sp^(1,2): the total expected sparsity-loss contribution of just latents 1 and 2 (E_h∼D[|z1|+|z2|]). p11, p10, p00: as defined in H2 above.
- **assumptions**: Same H1-H5 setup; additionally requires λ>0 (so the sparsity term actually matters to the total loss L=L_rec+λL_sp) and that the SAE 'learns or is constrained to' the δ-family of weight solutions (H5) — the proof shows this family is loss-favorable, not that gradient descent is guaranteed to FIND this particular family among all possible weight configurations.
- **derivation_steps**: Complete step-by-step: (1) tabulate |z1|+|z2| for each of the three permissible cases using the z1,z2 values already derived in Proposition 1; (2) take the probability-weighted expectation to get L_sp^(1,2) = p11(2−δ)+p10·1+p00·0, simplified to p11(2−δ)+p10 since p00's term is zero; (3) differentiate w.r.t. δ, noting p11,p10 are constants w.r.t. δ, giving dL_sp/dδ = d/dδ[2p11 − δp11 + p10] = −p11; (4) conclude: if p11>0 then the derivative is strictly negative, so L_sp^(1,2) is monotonically decreasing on [0,1], hence minimized at the boundary δ=1. Final synthesis (paper's own 'Conclusion' numbered list): since L_rec is UNAFFECTED by δ (Prop.1) while L_sp strictly favors δ→1, the total loss L=L_rec+λL_sp is minimized (among this weight family) at full absorption, so 'an optimization process like gradient descent... will favor increasing δ towards 1, thereby promoting feature absorption.'
- **worked_numbers**: L_sp^(1,2)(δ=0)=2p11+p10 (no absorption) vs. L_sp^(1,2)(δ=1)=p11+p10 (full absorption) — the gap between these two, exactly p11, IS the sparsity-loss reward for fully absorbing; it is proportional to the co-occurrence probability p11 and vanishes exactly when p11=0 (independent, non-hierarchical features), consistent with the paper's separate empirical finding (Fig.2a vs 2b) that independently-firing features ARE recovered perfectly while hierarchically co-occurring ones are not.
- **source**: Chanin et al. (2024)
- **locator**: Appendix A.2, 'Proposition 2' and 'Corollary', p.16-17
