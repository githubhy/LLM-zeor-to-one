## <a id="sec-A"></a>A ViT and encoder internals

<a id="p-a-vit-and-encoder-internals-1"></a><!-- para:a-vit-and-encoder-internals-1 --> **Depth tier:** headline

<a id="p-a-vit-and-encoder-internals-2"></a><!-- para:a-vit-and-encoder-internals-2 --> This appendix supplies the encoder-specific machinery that § <!-- secxref:2.3 -->[§2.3](fundamentals.md#sec-2.3) deferred: the multi-head bookkeeping, the pre-norm block's rationale, the GELU MLP, the class-token readout, and the projection head that turns a ViT into a CLIP/SigLIP encoder. The base scaled-dot-product attention is assumed; its first-principles derivation is in the companion [attention appendix](../llms-for-coding/appendix-a-qkv-first-principles.md).

<a id="p-a-vit-and-encoder-internals-3"></a><!-- para:a-vit-and-encoder-internals-3 --> **The one identity everything else costs against.** ViT reshapes an image into "a sequence of flattened 2D patches ... where $N = HW/P^2$ is the resulting number of patches, which also serves as the effective input sequence length for the Transformer" <!-- cite:2 -->[[2]](#ref-2). Because attention is quadratic in sequence length, and $N$ is quadratic in $1/P$, **compute is quartic in the inverse patch size** — the paper puts it as "the Transformer's sequence length is inversely proportional to the square of the patch size, thus models with smaller patch size are computationally more expensive" <!-- cite:2 -->[[2]](#ref-2). Concretely, at $224$ px a patch size of $16$ gives $N = 196$ and a patch size of $14$ gives $N = 256$; raising the input to $336$ px at patch $14$ gives $N = 576$, the figure <!-- secxref:F.5 -->[§F.5](appendix-f-audio-video.md#sec-F.5) multiplies by the frame count. Halving $P$ quadruples the tokens and so raises the cost of the **attention score matrix** roughly sixteenfold. Scope that carefully: only the $O(N^2)$ term moves by $16\times$; the per-token projections and the MLP are $O(N)$ and rise by $4\times$, and at the small patch counts typical of an encoder ($N$ in the low hundreds) those linear terms usually dominate the wall time. So end-to-end encoder cost rises by well under $16\times$ — the quartic is a statement about the term that eventually dominates, not about today's measured runtime. Every token-reduction method in <!-- secxref:8 -->[§8](inference-and-serving.md#sec-8) exists to buy back some part of that exponent.

### <a id="sec-A.0"></a>A.0 What the position embedding actually contributes

<a id="p-a0-what-the-position-embedding-actually-contributes-1"></a><!-- para:a0-what-the-position-embedding-actually-contributes-1 --> A patch sequence carries no geometry: flatten a grid and the transformer sees a *set*, so without a position embedding the model cannot tell a face from its scrambled parts. The natural expectation is therefore that a **2-D-aware** encoding — one that knows patch $(3,7)$ is above patch $(4,7)$ — should beat the "obviously wrong" 1-D index inherited from text. ViT tests this directly and reports the opposite: 1-D is used everywhere "since we have not observed significant performance gains from using more advanced 2D-aware position embeddings" <!-- cite:2 -->[[2]](#ref-2). Its ablation, on ViT-B/16 at ImageNet 5-shot linear:

| Position encoding | Score | vs. none | vs. 1-D |
|---|---|---|---|
| none | $0.61382$ | — | $-2.82$ pts |
| 1-D learned (the default) | $0.64206$ | $+2.82$ pts | — |
| 2-D learned ($X$/$Y$ halves concatenated) | $0.64001$ | $+2.62$ pts | $-0.20$ pts |
| relative | $0.64032$ | $+2.65$ pts | $-0.17$ pts |

<a id="p-a0-what-the-position-embedding-actually-contributes-2"></a><!-- para:a0-what-the-position-embedding-actually-contributes-2 --> The structure of the result is the finding: having *some* position encoding is worth $2.82$ points, and choosing *which* is worth at most $0.20$ — a ratio of about $14\times$ — with the geometry-aware options landing marginally **below** the naive one. The source's explanation is a resolution argument, and it is checkable: "since our Transformer encoder operates on patch-level inputs, as opposed to pixel-level, the differences in how to encode spatial information is less important ... the spatial dimensions are much smaller than the original pixel-level inputs, e.g., $14 \times 14$ as opposed to $224 \times 224$, and learning to represent the spatial relations in this resolution is equally easy for these different positional encoding strategies" <!-- cite:2 -->[[2]](#ref-2). A $14 \times 14$ grid has $196$ positions; recovering two dimensions of structure from $196$ learnable vectors is an easy fit, so the inductive bias a 2-D scheme supplies is one the 1-D scheme simply learns. *[established]*

<a id="p-a0-what-the-position-embedding-actually-contributes-3"></a><!-- para:a0-what-the-position-embedding-actually-contributes-3 --> Two cautions before generalizing this. The differences among the three encodings are within a few tenths of a point at a single setting with no dispersion reported, so the ordering among them should not be read as a ranking at all — the honest claim is that they are indistinguishable here, not that 1-D is best. And the resolution argument *predicts its own breakdown*: the whole case rests on the patch grid being small, so it should weaken as grids grow. That is consistent with the fact that models operating at native or tiled high resolution did not stay with 1-D learned embeddings — Qwen2-VL replaces them with 2-D RoPE precisely to handle variable-resolution inputs (<!-- secxref:F.6 -->[§F.6](appendix-f-audio-video.md#sec-F.6)). The ablation is evidence about $14 \times 14$ grids, not a general law about position encodings.

### <a id="sec-A.1"></a>A.1 Multi-head self-attention, the encoder version

<a id="p-a1-multi-head-self-attention-the-encoder-version-1"></a><!-- para:a1-multi-head-self-attention-the-encoder-version-1 --> Inside the MSA sublayer of the <!-- secxref:2.3 -->[§2.3](fundamentals.md#sec-2.3) encoder block, the patch sequence $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_v}$ is processed by $h$ attention heads in parallel. Each head $i$ projects the sequence to its own queries, keys, and values, runs scaled-dot-product attention, and the heads are concatenated and mixed by an output projection:

<a id="eq-1"></a><!-- eq:A-1 -->
$$
\mathrm{head}_i = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{(\mathbf{z}W_Q^i)(\mathbf{z}W_K^i)^\top}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right)\mathbf{z}W_V^i, \qquad \mathrm{MSA}(\mathbf{z}) = \big[\mathrm{head}_1;\cdots;\mathrm{head}_h\big]W_O \tag{1}
$$

<a id="p-a1-multi-head-self-attention-the-encoder-version-2"></a><!-- para:a1-multi-head-self-attention-the-encoder-version-2 --> with $W_Q^i, W_K^i, W_V^i\in\mathbb{R}^{d_v\times d_h}$, per-head width $d_h = d_v/h$, and $W_O\in\mathbb{R}^{d_v\times d_v}$. Two encoder-specific points distinguish this from a language model's attention. First, there is **no causal mask**: row $j$ of the softmax attends over *all* $N$ patches, left and right, because an image has no temporal arrow — the encoder is bidirectional (<!-- secxref:2.3 -->[§2.3](fundamentals.md#sec-2.3)). Second, multiple heads let the encoder run several distinct "matched filters" at once — one head may track texture continuity, another long-range object structure — and the output projection $W_O$ recombines them; the per-head dimension $d_h$ is small precisely so that $h$ heads fit in the same $d_v$ budget.

### <a id="sec-A.2"></a>A.2 Pre-norm and the residual stream

<a id="p-a2-pre-norm-and-the-residual-stream-1"></a><!-- para:a2-pre-norm-and-the-residual-stream-1 --> The <!-- secxref:2.3 -->[§2.3](fundamentals.md#sec-2.3) encoder block places layer normalization *before* each sublayer and adds the residual *after* — the pre-norm arrangement. The reason is gradient stability in deep stacks: with pre-norm, the residual path from input to output is an unobstructed identity (each block adds $\mathrm{MSA}(\mathrm{LN}(\cdot))$ to the running stream rather than normalizing the sum), so the gradient reaches early layers undiminished and very deep encoders train without the warmup gymnastics post-norm needs. The running sum down the stack is the *residual stream*: each block reads the current representation, computes a correction, and writes it back, so a patch token's vector accumulates evidence layer by layer. The class token (<!-- secref:A.4 -->[§A.4](#sec-A.4)) is one more lane of this stream, distinguished only by carrying no patch content at the input.

### <a id="sec-A.3"></a>A.3 The MLP sublayer

<a id="p-a3-the-mlp-sublayer-1"></a><!-- para:a3-the-mlp-sublayer-1 --> The second sublayer is a position-wise two-layer MLP with a GELU nonlinearity, applied identically to every token: $\mathrm{MLP}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathrm{GELU}(\mathbf{x}W_1 + b_1)W_2 + b_2$, with $W_1\in\mathbb{R}^{d_v\times d_{\mathrm{ff}}}$ expanding to a wider hidden width ($d_{\mathrm{ff}}$ is typically $4d_v$) and $W_2$ projecting back. Where attention *mixes* information across tokens, the MLP *transforms* each token's features in place; the alternation of the two — mix, then transform, repeated $L$ times — is the transformer's basic rhythm, unchanged from text to vision. GELU, a smooth gated approximation to ReLU, is the activation ViT inherits from the language-model lineage it deliberately copies.

### <a id="sec-A.4"></a>A.4 Class token, patch tokens, and resolution change

<a id="p-a4-class-token-patch-tokens-and-resolution-change-1"></a><!-- para:a4-class-token-patch-tokens-and-resolution-change-1 --> The prepended class token's final-layer state $\mathbf{z}_L^0$, after a last layer norm, is the single-vector image representation $\mathbf{y}$ (<!-- secxref:2.2 -->[§2.2](fundamentals.md#sec-2.2)). It works as a readout register: carrying no patch content itself, its only way to become informative is to attend to the patches, so after $L$ layers it holds a learned pooling of the whole image. This is the vector CLIP aligns to text (§ <!-- secxref:2.4 -->[§2.4](fundamentals.md#sec-2.4)) — and, as <!-- secxref:2.4 -->[§2.4](fundamentals.md#sec-2.4) noted, its single-vector nature is a bottleneck, which is why instruction-tuned VLMs feed the LLM the *grid* of patch tokens $\mathbf{z}_L^{1:N}$ instead. Changing input resolution changes $N$, and because the learned 1-D position embeddings $\mathbf{E}_{\text{pos}}$ were trained for one specific grid, they must be **2-D interpolated** to the new grid before fine-tuning at a higher resolution — the one place a 2-D spatial prior is manually reinjected into an otherwise shape-agnostic model, and a routine source of fine-tuning bugs.

### <a id="sec-A.5"></a>A.5 From ViT to a CLIP/SigLIP encoder

<a id="p-a5-from-vit-to-a-clipsiglip-encoder-1"></a><!-- para:a5-from-vit-to-a-clipsiglip-encoder-1 --> A bare ViT outputs $d_v$-dimensional features; turning it into a *language-aligned* encoder adds a projection into the joint embedding space and a pooling choice. CLIP <!-- cite:2 -->[[2]](#ref-2) takes the class-token representation, applies a single **linear projection** $W_I$ into the shared $d_e$-dimensional space, and $L2$-normalizes (<!-- secxref:2.4 -->[§2.4](fundamentals.md#sec-2.4)); it deliberately avoids a nonlinear projection head, which the authors found co-adapts to the image-only self-supervised setting and does not help here. SigLIP <!-- cite:15 -->[[15]](#ref-15) keeps the same ViT backbone and projection structure and changes only the *loss* (the sigmoid objective of § <!-- secxref:2.5 -->[§2.5](fundamentals.md#sec-2.5)), which is why a "SigLIP encoder" is a drop-in replacement for a "CLIP encoder" everywhere in this survey — same architecture, cheaper training, language-aligned $d_v$-dimensional patch features that a connector (§ <!-- secxref:3.3 -->[§3.3](architecture-building-blocks.md#sec-3.3)) maps into the LLM. The audio encoder of § <!-- secxref:7.1 -->[§7.1](modality-breadth.md#sec-7.1) is the same template over a spectrogram rather than a patch grid.
